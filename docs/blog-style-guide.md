# AV Conference, LED Wall & Live Events Blog Writing Style Guide

> Source: competitor content audit of Irish AV/event-production companies (window
> 12 Aug 2024 – 12 Aug 2026), commissioned Aug 2026. Keyword mapping for each
> post lives in [blog-content-plan.md](blog-content-plan.md) — that closes the
> "no keyword list supplied" gap this guide flags.

**Use:** editorial guidance for conference AV, LED walls/screens, hybrid events and live-event production.

## Editorial position

Write as an experienced production partner, not an equipment catalogue.

The strongest market position combines four qualities:

- **Technically credible:** explain enough detail to help a buyer make a better decision.
- **Calm and practical:** reduce uncertainty around live-event risk, planning and specification.
- **Experience-led:** connect technology choices to audience experience, presenter confidence and event outcomes.
- **Commercial without being pushy:** answer the buyer's question first; earn the enquiry afterwards.

Avoid generic claims such as "cutting-edge solutions", "unforgettable experiences" and "state-of-the-art technology" unless immediately supported by a specific example, specification, result or process.

## Recommended tone

Aim for:

- confident, calm and expert;
- clear rather than overly technical;
- helpful and candid about trade-offs;
- specific to Irish venues, weather, event formats and procurement realities where relevant;
- human enough to sound like a production expert speaking to an organiser;
- outcome-led: reliability, sightlines, intelligibility, engagement, accessibility, run-of-show confidence and budget control.

A useful tone test:

> "Would a senior event manager trust this advice enough to use it in a production meeting?"

### Tone to avoid

Avoid:

- hard-sell language in the opening paragraphs;
- excessive superlatives;
- unexplained AV jargon;
- long equipment lists with no decision-making context;
- repeating the same keyword unnaturally;
- promising "flawless" delivery without explaining the process that reduces risk;
- writing every article as "Top 5 trends" or "Why AV matters".

## Target audience

Write primarily for people who commission, influence or manage event production:

- event and conference managers;
- marketing and communications teams;
- corporate event owners;
- venue managers;
- agencies and professional conference organisers;
- operations/procurement teams;
- hospitality and sports venues considering LED;
- organisations running AGMs, town halls, awards, launches and hybrid events.

Assume the reader understands events but may not understand pixel pitch, signal distribution, rigging, redundancy, camera workflows or broadcast audio.

## Vocabulary

### Prefer

Use precise, buyer-relevant language such as:

**Planning and production:** event brief, technical specification, site visit, venue constraints, run of show, rehearsal, show caller, cueing, production schedule, load-in, de-rig, breakout rooms, technical crew, contingency, redundancy.

**Audio:** speech intelligibility, microphone choice, radio microphones, lectern microphone, panel discussion, foldback, PA coverage, audio mix, remote contributor audio.

**Video and LED:** LED wall, LED screen, pixel pitch, viewing distance, brightness, nits, image processing, content resolution, aspect ratio, camera feed, confidence monitor, presentation playback, IMAG, display size.

**Conference and hybrid:** keynote, panel, Q&A, remote speaker, livestream, webcast, hybrid audience, recording, captioning, hearing assistance, interpretation, audience engagement.

**Commercial and outcome language:** budget, scope, specification, total cost, value, audience experience, presenter confidence, reliability, accessibility, measurable impact, sponsor visibility, brand consistency.

### Use sparingly

Use these only when supported by evidence or a concrete example:

- seamless;
- immersive;
- unforgettable;
- world-class;
- cutting-edge;
- state-of-the-art;
- game-changing;
- next-level;
- wow factor.

## Sounding human (hard rules — these override everything else)

AI-written text has fingerprints. Under a named byline they destroy
credibility. These are mechanical limits, checked before publication:

- **Em dashes ( — ): maximum one per post, ideally zero.** Replace with a
  full stop, a comma, or brackets. This is the single biggest tell.
- **Semicolons: zero.** A real production engineer writes two sentences.
- **Rule-of-three lists ("fast, reliable and affordable"): break them.**
  Use two items, or four, or one. Constant triplets read as generated.
- **No "isn't just X — it's Y" constructions.** Say the thing directly.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Real writing has a five-word sentence next to a
  twenty-five-word one, an aside in brackets, an occasional sentence
  starting with And or But. Uniformly polished medium-length sentences are
  a tell in themselves.
- **Let it be slightly rough.** A plain flat sentence ("Wind is the thing
  that catches people out.") beats a balanced elegant one. Contractions
  everywhere. The test: read a paragraph aloud — would Graham say it on the
  phone to a client?

## Sentence and paragraph length

Target an average sentence length of **14–20 words**.

Use a mix of:

- short sentences for emphasis;
- medium sentences for explanation;
- occasional longer sentences when a technical qualification genuinely needs it.

Keep most paragraphs to **2–4 sentences**. On mobile, avoid large blocks of text.

For technical sections, introduce the concept in plain English before giving specifications.

Example:

> Pixel pitch is the distance between LED pixels. A smaller pitch gives a sharper image at close viewing distances, but it also increases cost. The right choice depends on how close the audience will be to the screen.

## Recommended post length

Choose length according to search intent, not a fixed house word count.

| Content type | Recommended length | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Quick answer / timely post | 600–900 words | Answer one focused question quickly |
| Practical guide / checklist | 1,000–1,500 words | Capture problem-solving and planning intent |
| Technical buyer guide | 1,400–2,200 words | Explain specifications, trade-offs, costs and decisions |
| Case study | 700–1,200 words | Show challenge, approach, technical solution and result |
| Pillar guide | 2,000–3,000 words | Own a broad topic and internally link to supporting articles |

Do not inflate a post simply to reach a word count. A complete 900-word answer is better than a repetitive 1,800-word article.

## Heading structure

Use one H1 only.

Recommended structure:

```
# H1: Clear buyer question or benefit-led headline

Opening: 80–140 words
- identify the reader's problem;
- state what the article will answer;
- establish practical relevance.

## H2: The key decision or problem
## H2: What you need to know
### H3: Technical factor one
### H3: Technical factor two
### H3: Technical factor three
## H2: What this means for [event type / venue type]
## H2: Common mistakes or risks
## H2: Practical checklist / specification table
## H2: Frequently asked questions
## H2: Planning your next event  ← short, relevant CTA
```

For case studies:

```
# [Client/Event]: [Outcome or production challenge]

## The brief
## The venue and technical constraints
## The production approach
## The AV solution
## How the show was delivered
## The result
## What event organisers can learn from it
```

The final "what organisers can learn" section turns a portfolio piece into useful search content.

## SEO approach

Optimise around the reader's question first.

For each article:

1. Select one primary search intent.
2. Use one primary keyword/topic in the H1, title tag and naturally in the opening.
3. Use closely related terms in H2s where they genuinely help navigation.
4. Add internal links to the relevant service page, one or two related guides and a relevant case study.
5. Add a concise FAQ section only when the questions are genuinely useful.
6. Use descriptive image filenames and alt text.
7. Include location terms such as Ireland, Dublin, Cork or Galway only where the service/search intent is geographically relevant.
8. Avoid stuffing "Ireland" or "Dublin" into every heading.
9. Refresh time-sensitive pricing, technology and "trends" articles annually.
10. Where the article includes costs, specifications, standards or safety advice, state assumptions and date the information.

- **[Service] in Ireland: What Event Organisers Need to Know**

- **How to Plan [AV Requirement] for a [Conference/Awards/AGM]**

- **A Technical Guide to [LED/Hybrid/Audio Topic]**

## Meta description examples

Keep meta descriptions roughly **140–160 characters** where possible, with a concrete benefit and natural language.

**Conference AV**
> Planning a conference in Ireland? Learn how to specify sound, screens, lighting, streaming and technical crew before you request AV quotes.

**LED walls**
> Choosing an LED wall for an event? Compare pixel pitch, brightness, viewing distance, indoor/outdoor requirements and rental options.

**Live events**
> A practical guide to live-event AV production, covering staging, sound, lighting, video, rehearsals, crew and contingency planning.

**Hybrid events**
> Learn what a reliable hybrid-event setup needs, from remote speakers and camera feeds to audio, captioning, streaming and backup plans.

**AV costs**
> Understand what drives event AV costs in Ireland, including equipment, crew, venue access, rehearsal time, LED, streaming and production complexity.

## Calls to action

CTAs should match the article's stage of intent.

### Low-intent / educational

- **Download the conference AV briefing checklist.**
- **See the LED wall specification guide.**
- **Read a conference case study with a similar room format.**
- **Compare indoor and outdoor LED options.**

### Mid-intent / planning

- **Send us your venue, audience size and event format and we'll help you sense-check the AV brief.**
- **Share your floor plan and we'll recommend screen size, viewing distance and placement.**
- **Book a technical site visit before you finalise the production specification.**
- **Ask us to review your conference run of show for AV dependencies.**

### High-intent / commercial

- **Request an AV production quote.**
- **Book a technical consultation.**
- **Talk to our production team about your event.**
- **Check LED wall availability for your dates.**

Avoid ending every article with exactly the same "contact us today" line. Match the CTA to the problem the reader has just solved.

## Image and visual guidance

AV is inherently visual; every substantial article should demonstrate the advice rather than rely on generic stock imagery.

### Minimum visual set for a guide

- one strong hero image from a real event or installation;
- one annotated technical image, diagram or room layout where useful;
- one close-up that demonstrates the equipment/specification being discussed;
- one finished-event image showing audience sightlines or production context.

For a 1,200–1,800 word guide, **3–6 useful visuals** is a good working range.

### Preferred visual formats

Prioritise: real LED-wall installations; before/after venue transformations; wide shots showing screen scale and sightlines; stage and room layouts; signal-flow diagrams; annotated photos explaining pixel pitch, camera position or microphone coverage; screenshots of virtual/hybrid event outputs; short embedded video clips or showreels; charts comparing options, costs or viewing distances; case-study galleries.

Avoid generic pictures of mixing desks unless the image teaches something.

### Alt text

Describe the image accurately and specifically.

Weak:
> LED screen Ireland

Better:
> Indoor LED wall behind a conference stage with twin lecterns and audience seating in Dublin

Do not force keywords into alt text where they do not describe the image.

## Evidence and credibility

Where possible, turn claims into proof.

Instead of:
> We provide reliable conference AV.

Write:
> The production plan includes a technical rehearsal, presentation checks, spare radio microphones and backup playback so common show-day failures are addressed before doors open.

Useful evidence types: named venues and event formats; equipment/specifications when relevant; audience size; screen dimensions; pixel pitch and brightness; number of rooms or remote contributors; load-in constraints; sustainability measures; accessibility provisions; client quote; measurable result; lesson learned.

Do not publish confidential client information or unsupported performance claims.

## Content pillars

A balanced editorial programme covers six clusters:

1. **Conference AV** — briefing & checklists; audio & presenter support; breakouts & room planning
2. **LED Walls & Screens** — pixel pitch & viewing distance; indoor vs outdoor; content & processing
3. **Live Event Production** — staging, lighting & show flow; crew & rehearsals; contingency
4. **Hybrid & Accessibility** — remote contributors; captioning & hearing access; streaming workflow
5. **Costs, Risk & Procurement** — budget & cost drivers; supplier comparison; technical specification
6. **Case Studies & Venues** — venue-specific lessons; challenge / solution / result

## Editorial workflow

1. **Start with a real buyer question.** Use sales calls, site visits, production debriefs and quote-stage objections.
2. **Choose the search intent.** Informational, comparison, cost, troubleshooting or commercial.
3. **Interview a subject-matter expert.** Capture details a generic copywriter would not know.
4. **Build the outline.** Answer the main question early, then expand into decisions and trade-offs.
5. **Add proof.** Use a real event, venue, specification, diagram or calculation.
6. **Write for scanning.** Clear H2s, short paragraphs, tables and checklists.
7. **Add internal links and CTA.** Link to the next useful action, not merely the homepage.
8. **Technical fact-check.** Have production/engineering review specifications and safety language.
9. **Publish and distribute.** Website first, then adapt to LinkedIn, email and short-form social.
10. **Refresh.** Revisit high-value guides after equipment, pricing, standards or market conditions change.

## Recommended publishing cadence

A strong starting cadence is **two substantive posts per month**, supported by **one case study or project note per month** when project permissions allow.

Suggested monthly mix: one search-led practical/technical guide; one audience/problem-led article; one case study or venue/project story. Quarterly, add one larger pillar article or downloadable checklist.

Consistency is more valuable than a short burst of generic weekly posts.

## Distribution

Treat each article as a source asset. For every strong blog post, consider creating: one LinkedIn post from the commercial lesson; one LinkedIn carousel from a checklist or comparison; one short video from the technical expert; one email/newsletter section; two or three image-led social posts from the project photography; one sales enablement link for proposals or follow-up emails.

Case studies should also feed venue pages, sector pages and pitch decks.

## Author and trust signals

Where possible, publish expert content under a named author.

> **Name — Role**
> Two sentences on relevant conference, LED, broadcast or live-event experience.

For high-stakes technical articles, add:

> **Technical review:** Name, role.

Author identity helps distinguish first-hand production expertise from generic SEO copy.

## Comments and engagement

Do not depend on blog comments as the main engagement mechanism. The stronger conversion path is: article → useful CTA → LinkedIn/email distribution → direct question, briefing form or site-visit request.

Measure qualified enquiries, assisted conversions, time on page, downloads and organic visibility — not comments.

## Priority topic gaps to own

The strongest opportunities are topics competitors touch only lightly or inconsistently:

- detailed conference AV briefing/specification templates;
- presenter preparation and slide/content handover;
- LED content design, aspect ratios and content workflows;
- accessibility as a full production discipline;
- contingency and redundancy explained for non-technical organisers;
- venue access, power, rigging, acoustics and sightline planning;
- transparent cost drivers without pretending there is one universal package price;
- measurable event outcomes and sponsor/brand value;
- technical debriefs that explain what changed during a real production;
- procurement guidance: how to compare AV quotes on a like-for-like basis.
