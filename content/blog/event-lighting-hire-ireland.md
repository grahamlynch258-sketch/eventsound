The kit is the easy part. Uplighters, moving heads, washes and a control desk do much the same jobs wherever you put them. What decides your actual spec is the room. A hotel ballroom with a house rig, a low-ceilinged meeting room, a heritage hall where nothing can be fixed to the walls, and a marquee in a field are four different problems, even with the same guest list and the same brief.

So this guide reads the venue first. What each piece of kit does and where it has to go, then the room itself (power, rigging height, floor space and access times), then what different kinds of venue tend to call for. Settle all that before anyone quotes you.

## The kit, and what each piece asks of a room

Hiring it is handled through our [lighting hire and design service](/services/lighting-design/), quoted all-inclusive: equipment, delivery, installation, on-site operation and breakdown in one figure, with no hidden line items. Within that, four kinds of kit do most of the work.

### LED uplighting

Uplighters sit on the floor and throw colour up walls, columns, drapes and set pieces. They're the fastest way to make a plain function room feel like your event rather than the last one, and they can hold one colour all night or change as the evening moves. Being LED, they run cool and draw very little power.

### Moving heads

Moving heads are motorised fixtures that pan, tilt, zoom and change colour from the control desk. One fixture can spotlight a speaker or texture a dance floor. It can chase through a band set too, without anyone climbing a ladder. If the budget only stretches to one upgrade, a handful of moving heads adds more flexibility than any amount of static kit. The catch is height. They have to sit well above head level, on the venue's bars or on truss you bring in, so a low ceiling is the first thing to check.

### Wash and stage lighting

Faces come first. Even, warm light across the lectern, stage or top table is what makes speakers legible from the back of the room and presentable on camera. If your event involves anyone standing up to talk, fund this layer before anything decorative.

### Specials: gobos, pin spots and follow spots

Gobo projection puts a logo or pattern onto a floor or wall. Pin spots pick out table centrepieces once the house lights drop. Follow spots track a winner or performer live across a stage, and architectural lighting handles building exteriors and facades. None are essential. Each earns its place at the right event.

Everything above runs from a proper control desk (we use Chamsys), with an operator changing the room as the night moves. For how a designer turns this kit into states and cues, see our guide to [conference and awards night lighting design](/blog/event-lighting-design-guide/).

## Reading the room: power, height and access

### Power

LED kit is far less power-hungry than the older tungsten gear it replaced. A typical uplighting package runs from standard sockets. Larger rigs with moving heads want dedicated circuits, and big shows may need three-phase distribution. None of this is a problem. It just needs to be known before show day rather than during load-in.

### Height and somewhere to put it

Uplighters need a clear run of wall. Moving heads and washes need height: either floor stands and truss, which take floor space, or the venue's own rigging points and bars. Ceiling height, beams and chandeliers all shape what's possible, and in venues with a house rig we can often add to what's installed rather than duplicate it.

### Access and time

The unglamorous questions decide the schedule. Where does the van unload? Is there a lift, or three flights of stairs? When is the room free for setup, and is there any window when it can go dark for focusing? Site visits are free for confirmed bookings, and one walk-through answers all of this in a single pass.

## What different venues call for

- **Hotel ballrooms, for gala dinners and awards:** uplighting for the room, wash light on the stage, pin spots on the tables, and a moving head or follow spot for the walk-ups.
- **Conference and meeting rooms:** front light and screen balance first, brand-colour uplighting second, effects a distant third.
- **Blank spaces, for launches and brand events:** gobo projection, saturated colour and architectural lighting to make the building part of the message.
- **Marquees:** there's no house rig and no house power, so everything must be brought in. Flag a marquee early in the conversation.

If a big screen is part of the staging, say so when you enquire. Screen brightness changes the lighting spec, and a projector changes it far more than an LED wall does, because projection wants the room darker and front light kept off the screen entirely. Our comparison of [an LED wall or a projector](/blog/led-wall-or-projector/) covers which one a given room can carry.

## Common mistakes when you light a venue

- **Booking uplighters and forgetting the stage.** Colour everywhere, speaker in the dark.
- **Assuming the venue "has lights".** House lighting is general-purpose. It won't follow your running order.
- **Leaving power to chance.** The five-minute power conversation with the venue prevents the worst load-in surprises.
- **Describing brand colours in words.** Send the exact reference, not "our sort of teal".
- **Skipping the operator.** A rig nobody runs holds one look all night, which is most of the budget wasted.
- **Booking lighting last.** Once staging and screens are locked, the lighting is working around decisions it should have shaped.

## The venue checklist before you ask for a quote

- Date, venue and room
- Guest numbers and layout: banquet rounds, theatre rows or standing
- Event format, and the two or three moments that matter most
- Brand colours as exact references
- Stage, screen or video wall plans
- What the venue offers: house rig, rigging points, power
- Access times and any load-in restrictions
- Whether the event is filmed or photographed
