To choose an event production company, ask ten things before you sign anything: who will be on site on the day, what the quote covers, whether they've done your type of event, who owns the equipment, whether they'll visit the venue, what rehearsal you get, what happens if something fails, how they work with the venue's own systems, who your one contact is, and how load-in and breakdown are timed. A good company answers each one plainly and in detail. A weak one goes vague. Below, each question comes with why it matters, what a good answer sounds like and what should make you pause. If the term is new to you, [event production](/services/event-production/) simply means one team plans and runs the whole technical side of your event, from sound and lighting to screens and staging.

## Before you start asking

Send every company the same short brief and ask the same questions in writing. Written answers are easier to compare a week later, and they show you who bothers to be specific.

It also helps to know whether you need full production or just equipment and someone to run it. Our guide on [AV hire or full event production](/blog/av-hire-or-event-production/) explains the difference. If you only need a screen and a microphone for a board meeting, you're asking a different set of questions.

## 1. Who will actually be on site on the day

This matters because the people who sell you the job are not always the people who run it. A polished meeting with a salesperson tells you nothing about the technician standing at the mixing desk when your chief executive walks on.

A good answer names the roles. You'll hear how many people are coming, what each one does and who is in charge on the floor. A warning sign is "we'll assign the crew nearer the time" for a large event, or no clear answer on who operates what.

For us, every AV hire includes a technician on site, and larger events get a multi-person crew. We're also owner-led, with 35+ years of event production experience behind the planning.

## 2. Is the quote all-inclusive, and what exactly does it cover

Two quotes for the same event can look very different because one leaves things out. Delivery, installation, an operator and breakdown are the usual gaps. If they aren't written down, you'll meet them later as extras.

A good answer lists what's covered in plain words: the equipment, getting it there, setting it up, running it during the event and taking it away. A warning sign is a quote that is just a list of kit, or a company that says "we'll see what else comes up".

Our quotes are all-inclusive. They cover equipment, delivery, installation, on-site operation and breakdown, with no hidden fees and no line-item pricing. If you're comparing several, our [guide to comparing AV quotes](/blog/compare-av-quotes-guide/) shows how to put them side by side.

## 3. Have they done this type of event, and can they show you

A company that runs excellent music festivals may never have handled a council awards night with a teleprompter and nine speakers. The skills overlap but they aren't identical. Experience with your format means fewer surprises.

A good answer is specific. They name events like yours, describe what the job involved and offer photos, a case study or a reference you can call. A warning sign is a long list of impressive logos with nothing about what the company actually did at each one.

Here's what we can point to. We delivered full event production for the Explore Fingal launch at the Grand Hotel Malahide. At the Swords Castle Summer Concerts, with 8,000+ attendees per night, we supply the outdoor LED screen and event production. We also provided conference production for the PRISM Immersive Technology Summit at the Mac Anna Theatre, and an LED wall and lighting for the Mayors Awards in Dublin. Our clients include Fingal County Council and Louth County Council.

## 4. Do they own the equipment, or hire it in

Neither answer is automatically wrong. Plenty of good companies hire in specialist kit for a particular job. What matters is that they tell you, because it affects who is responsible if something fails and how well the crew knows the gear.

A good answer is open. They tell you what they own, what they would bring in for your event and who would operate it. A warning sign is hesitation or annoyance when you ask, or a vague "we have access to everything".

We use our own equipment and crew. Put the question to every company you're considering and write down the answer. It tells you a lot about how the business is run.

## 5. Will they visit the venue before the event

Rooms look different on a floor plan than they do at 8am on the day. Power points, ceiling heights, loading doors, pillars blocking a sightline and a lift that only fits half your kit are all things a visit finds.

A good answer is a firm yes for anything beyond a simple job, with someone who will actually be responsible for the event doing the visiting. A warning sign is "we'll work it out from the photos" for a large or unfamiliar venue.

Our site visits are free for confirmed bookings.

## 6. Is there a rehearsal, and how much time does it get

Live events go wrong at the handover points: a presenter's slides won't open, a radio microphone is switched on in the wrong place, a video plays without sound. A rehearsal finds these while nobody is watching.

A good answer sets out when the rehearsal happens, who needs to be there and what gets tested. For example, every presenter's slides, every microphone and the video playback. A warning sign is "we'll do a quick sound check as people arrive", especially if your event has several speakers or any recorded content.

On our production jobs, rehearsal is part of the day, after load-in and installation. Ask any company what rehearsal time you'd need to give them in the venue, because that affects your booking.

## 7. What is the backup if something fails

Every piece of equipment can fail. A professional company has already thought about which failures would hurt most and what it does about them. You aren't asking for a promise that nothing will go wrong. You're asking what happens when it does.

A good answer is concrete. Spare radio microphones, a second playback source, a clear plan for what happens if a screen or a feed drops. A warning sign is "that never happens to us".

We carry backup equipment on site for critical systems. For streamed and hybrid events, we use redundant internet, a wired connection plus bonded cellular, and we recommend a minimum 10Mbps dedicated upload at the venue. Ask any company for the equivalent detail for the part of your event that matters most.

## 8. How do they work with the venue and its house systems

Many hotels, theatres and conference centres have their own sound, lighting or screens. Sometimes you'll want to use them. Sometimes the company needs to work around them. Either way, a supplier who ignores the house rig can create arguments with the venue on the day.

A good answer shows they've thought about it. They ask what the venue has, speak to the venue's technical contact and are clear about what they'll bring themselves. A warning sign is a company that has never asked the venue anything before quoting.

We work with existing house rigs in venues across Ireland, so it's a normal part of how we plan.

## 9. Who is my single point of contact

When something changes the week before, you want one person to ring. If you have to chase the sound supplier, the lighting supplier and the screen supplier separately, you've become the producer without being asked.

A good answer names one person who knows your event, will pass on changes to the crew and will still be reachable on the day. A warning sign is a company where you speak to someone different every time, or where nobody can tell you who takes decisions.

We're owner-led, which keeps that line short.

## 10. How do load-in and breakdown work

Load-in is when the equipment arrives and goes up. Breakdown is when it comes down and leaves. Both need agreed times with the venue, and both can collide with other events, including a lunch that finishes late or a wedding in the next room.

A good answer is a schedule. They tell you when they need access, how long they need and what has to be clear before they start. They also say when they'll be out. A warning sign is "we'll just turn up and sort it out", particularly at hotels, schools or outdoor sites where access is limited.

Installation and breakdown are both included in our quotes, so there's no separate conversation about who takes the kit down at midnight.

## What to send when you ask for a quote

The better your brief, the more useful the answers. Send each company the same things:

- the date, venue and expected audience size
- the type of event and what it needs to achieve
- the running order, even a rough draft
- any speakers, performers or presentations that need technical support
- what the venue already has, if you know
- the times you can get into the room and when you must be out
- anything already booked or fixed, such as a stage or a screen

You don't need a technical specification. Writing that is part of what you're asking them to do. If the running order isn't final, say so. A draft is far more useful than silence.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many companies should I ask for a quote?**
Two or three is usually enough. More than that and you spend your time comparing documents that look alike. Ask each the same ten questions in the same order so the answers line up.

**When should I contact a production company?**
As soon as you have a date and a venue. Earlier is better for larger events, because the company can shape the plan with you rather than fitting equipment to decisions that are already made. If your date is close, say so up front and ask what they can realistically deliver.

**Does the company need to know my venue already?**
No. A good company can plan for a room it hasn't worked in before, which is what the site visit and the questions about house systems are for. Knowing the venue is a bonus, not a requirement.

**Should I choose a company in my own county?**
Not necessarily. We're based in Drogheda and work across Ireland, including Dublin, Cork, Galway, Belfast and Limerick. What matters is that they can cover your date, visit your venue and tell you plainly who will be there.
