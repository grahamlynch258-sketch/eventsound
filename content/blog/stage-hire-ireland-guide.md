Stage hire in Ireland comes down to five decisions: indoor or outdoor, how big, how high, what safety kit the platform needs, and who installs it. Get those right and the stage disappears into the event, which is exactly what a good stage should do. Get them wrong and every sightline, photograph and awards walk-up pays for it.

This guide works through each decision in turn: what a hired stage actually includes, how to size a platform for speakers, panels and bands, why height matters more than most organisers expect, and the safety features that separate a professional build from a stack of decks. It applies whether you are staging a conference in a hotel ballroom or a summer show in a field.

## Indoor or outdoor: the first decision

The same TUV-certified modular system builds both, but the two jobs are planned differently.

Indoors, the environment is on your side: a level floor, shelter and house power. The constraints are physical — door widths, lifts and corridors on the load-in route, and a ceiling that has to clear the platform, a standing presenter and whatever set or screen goes behind them. Measure the route and the room before you settle on a stage size.

Outdoors, ground, weather and wind take over the plan. Wind is what stops shows, rain is what soaks kit, and the ground decides whether the build is quick or slow. ## What size stage do you need?

Size the platform for what happens on it, then check the room or site can take it.

### Footprint: size it for the action

| What's on stage | Typical platform | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|
| Lectern and one speaker | 3m × 2m | Allow space to step away from the lectern |
| Panel discussion (four or five people) | 6m × 3m | Chairs and a coffee table swallow depth fast |
| Awards presentations | 6m × 4m | Presenter, winner and photographer all fit in shot |
| Live band or DJ | 6m × 4m upwards | A drum kit alone claims roughly 2m × 2m |
| School show, choir or dance group | 8m × 4m upwards | Large casts often work better with tiered rows |

Treat these as rule-of-thumb starting points rather than specifications. The running order settles the final footprint: a conference stage that also hosts a five-piece band at the evening dinner is sized for the band, not the lectern.

### Height: size it for the audience

Height is a sightline calculation, and it is the dimension organisers most often under-specify. On a flat floor with seated rows, platforms of roughly 600mm to 1m keep a presenter visible past the first few rows. Standing audiences outdoors need higher again — larger outdoor stages run well over a metre so the back of the crowd keeps a view. In a small room with two rows of chairs, 400mm can be plenty.

Cameras change the sums too. If the event is filmed or streamed, a raised platform gives the lenses a clean eyeline over seated heads, which shows up in every frame of the recording.

### Don't forget what stands on it

Lecterns, screens, banner frames and set pieces all eat footprint and height. If a screen stands on the platform, its top edge has to clear the presenter and still fit under the ceiling. Brief the stage and the AV together so the heights are worked out once — not discovered clashing on the morning of the event.

## Uneven ground and awkward rooms

Floors lie. Lawns slope, courtyards are cobbled, and older halls are rarely as level as they look. Our modular staging stands on adjustable legs, so each leg is set to the ground beneath it and the deck above comes up level — which is what makes lawns, yards and heritage venues workable at all.

If the site is genuinely awkward — a stepped plaza, a steep lawn — say so early and send photos. Site visits are free for confirmed bookings, and ten minutes on site answers questions a floor plan never will.

## Stage safety: what a professional build includes

Staging is one of the few parts of an event that can genuinely hurt someone, so this is the place to be fussy about your supplier.

- **A certified structure.** Our modular staging is TUV-certified, indoors and out. Whoever you hire, ask for certification evidence — venues and insurers increasingly want to see it.
- **Safety rails.** Open edges at the back and sides should carry rails wherever the height or the activity warrants them. A seated panel three steps up is one conversation; a children's dance troupe on a metre-high platform is another.
- **Proper steps.** Steps with a handrail, positioned where people actually get on and off — awards guests in evening wear should never be hauling themselves onto decks.
- **Skirting.** Mostly cosmetic, but it closes in the underside, keeps stored cases and cable runs out of sight, and stops the front row studying the void beneath the drummer.
- **A crew that installs and checks it.** A stage is not flat-pack furniture. Ours arrives with the crew who build it, level it, check it and take it away afterwards.

Nothing on that list is exotic. But rails, steps and skirting are exactly the items that vanish from a cheap quote and reappear as extras — or worse, never appear at all.

## Finishing: skirting, pipe and drape and star cloth

A bare platform does the job; a finished one looks like it belongs to the event. Black skirting tidies the structure itself. Pipe and drape builds a clean backdrop behind the stage and hides the working areas — storage, cable runs, the walkway winners use to reach the steps. For gala dinners and awards nights, star cloth — black drape studded with points of light — gives the stage a backdrop that photographs far better than a bare function-room wall.

If the event carries branding, decide early what hangs where. Drape gives banners and set pieces a neutral canvas, and the stage reads as part of the event rather than furniture parked in front of it.

## Access, build time and what to check with your venue

Before any stage is priced, five questions are worth answering:

- What is the load-in route — doors, corridors, lifts, stairs?
- Where can a van or truck park, and how far is that from the room or site?
- When is the space available to build, and when must it be clear?
- What is the ceiling height over the stage position?
- Are there floor-protection rules — sprung floors, pitches, heritage surfaces?

Build time scales with size and access far more than most schedules allow. A small indoor platform is quick; a large outdoor build with a long carry is a different day's work. Answer the five questions above and a supplier can tell you honestly what the schedule needs.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you build a stage on sloping or uneven ground?**
Yes — adjustable legs exist for exactly that. Each leg sets to the ground beneath it and the platform comes up level. For steep or stepped sites, send photos early; site visits are free for confirmed bookings.

**Does a low stage still need safety rails?**
Not always. It depends on the height, who is on the platform and what they are doing up there. We specify rails on open edges wherever the drop or the activity warrants them — and many venues and insurers expect them regardless, so check before ruling them out.
