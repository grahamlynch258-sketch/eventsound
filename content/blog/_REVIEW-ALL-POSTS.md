# 1. How We Produced the Swords Castle Summer Concerts (8,000 People a Night)

> **File:** `content/blog/swords-castle-summer-concerts-production.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-08-17 · **Target:** `outdoor concert production` · **Tailoring spots:** 9

Eight thousand people a night in the grounds of a medieval castle makes for a brilliant concert. It also makes for an unforgiving production site. There's no house PA, no built-in power, no loading dock, and no shelter for equipment that can't handle Irish weather.

EventSound delivered the outdoor LED screen and [event production](/services/event-production/) for the Swords Castle Summer Concerts, where artists including Nathan Carter played to crowds of 8,000+ per night in 2025. This case study covers the brief, the constraints of a walled heritage site, and what outdoor concert production at this scale actually involves. Plus the lessons any organiser of a large outdoor event can take from it.

## The brief

The Swords Castle Summer Concerts bring major touring artists into the centre of Swords, north County Dublin. The core of the brief was simple to state and hard to deliver: every one of 8,000+ attendees should be able to see and follow the performance, including the people standing furthest from the stage.

At this scale a screen isn't decoration. Beyond the front section of a standing crowd the performer becomes a small figure in the distance, and the audience experience depends on the video as much as the sound.

## The venue and technical constraints

Swords Castle is a walled medieval site in the heart of Swords. As a concert setting it's atmospheric and genuinely distinctive. As a production site, it behaves nothing like an arena.

A heritage venue like this shapes the technical plan before a single case is loaded in:

- **Nothing attaches to the fabric of the site.** Screens and technical structures must be freestanding and ballasted rather than rigged from walls or permanent points.
- **There is no built-in event infrastructure.** Power, cabling routes and backstage facilities all arrive temporarily and must leave without a trace.
- **Access was never designed for trucks.** Load-in routes, vehicle sizes, timings and ground protection have to be planned rather than assumed.
- **Weather is a planning assumption, not a surprise.** The outdoor LED and the structure holding it up are specified for wind and rain from day one. Same goes for the power distribution.

## The production approach

Large outdoor shows are won or lost in the planning. Three principles drove the approach at Swords Castle.

**Specify from the site, not from a catalogue.** A screen that looks superb indoors can be unwatchable in direct summer daylight. An outdoor concert screen needs weather-rated panels, high brightness and a support structure engineered for wind. The site conditions drive the specification, not the other way around.

**Plan the build backwards from doors.** With thousands of people arriving at a fixed time, every technical system must be tested and signed off before gates open. The schedule works backwards from that point: screen built and tested, power proven under load, communications checked.

**Carry contingency on site.** With 8,000 people a night, there's no acceptable version of "we'll sort it tomorrow". Backup equipment for critical systems travels with the show, and faults get swapped out rather than repaired mid-event.

## What event organisers can learn from it

**Unusual venues can host major events. You just have to budget for what they lack.** A castle or a civic space has no event infrastructure. Power, screens, PA and access all arrive as temporary systems, and they belong in the budget from the first draft.

**The screen is the audience experience past the front section.** Specify screen size and brightness from the site layout and viewing distances, not from what looks impressive in a brochure.

**Weather-rating is a specification decision, not a show-day one.** By the time the forecast turns, the only protection that counts is the kind engineered in weeks earlier.

**Fixed deadlines should drive the schedule.** Doors don't move. Build the technical schedule backwards from them, with testing time protected rather than treated as slack.

**Contingency is part of the specification.** Backup equipment for critical systems is what turns a potential show-stopper into a footnote.

# 2. What Does LED Wall Hire Cost in Ireland? Price Per m², Crew and What the Quote Includes

> **File:** `content/blog/led-wall-hire-cost-ireland.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-08-20 · **Target:** `led wall hire cost ireland` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Here's the straight answer on LED wall hire cost in Ireland, as of August 2026: our published dry hire rate is €125 per square metre per day, so the panels for a typical 4m × 2.5m conference wall (10m²) come to €1,250 a day. For wider calibration, a straightforward single-room conference setup with sound and screens starts around €1,500. Those figures answer the first question but not the real one, because panels are only one line of a working quote. Crew, processing, power, rigging, transport and show duration decide the final number. This guide walks through each of them and prices a worked example properly. It also sets out what an all-inclusive [LED video wall](/services/led-video-walls/) quote should contain.

## The headline number: €125 per m² per day

LED walls are modular, so pricing by area is the honest unit. A wall twice the size uses twice the panels, and the structure and signal path scale with it. Per-square-metre pricing also makes quotes comparable. You can hold any supplier's figure against the same square metreage.

The multiplication is simple enough to do in your head:

| 4m × 2.5m | 10m² | €1,250 |
| 5m × 3m | 15m² | €1,875 |
| 6m × 4m | 24m² | €3,000 |

Be clear about what that rate is: a dry hire figure for the equipment itself. It's the right number for production companies and venues with their own LED crew, and the wrong number to budget against if you need the wall delivered, built, operated and removed. If you're still working out sizes in the first place, it helps to see one at scale: our [Swords Castle concerts case study](/blog/swords-castle-summer-concerts-production/) covers an outdoor build for 8,000 people a night.

## Dry hire or operated: two different purchases

**Dry hire** is the equipment: panels, structure and processing. Your crew handles everything on site, from the build through the show to the de-rig. It suits professional productions that already carry LED technicians.

**Operated hire** is the finished outcome: delivery, installation, commissioning, an operator through the show and breakdown afterwards, quoted as one all-inclusive figure with no hidden fees. Most conference and event organisers want this one, and everything below describes what that single figure absorbs.

## What moves a real quote beyond the panel price

### Crew and build time

A wall has to be assembled, commissioned, operated and taken down, and the labour scales with size and access. A 10m² backdrop with a loading bay beside the room is a light build. A bigger wall up a staircase is not. Larger shows run multi-crew (Ronan and Mark join for those), and late-night de-rigs add hours, so share the venue schedule early.

### Processing and playback

The processor is the wall's brain. It maps content onto the canvas, scales each source and switches between them. Bigger walls and camera-fed shows need more processing and a more deliberate signal path, including spares on anything the show can't survive losing. Small indoor walls run happily from house circuits. Larger builds need three-phase supplies and distribution to match. None of it is exotic, but it must be confirmed against your actual venue rather than assumed. That's one reason site visits are free for confirmed bookings, and worth taking.

### Ground support or rigging

Most indoor walls stand on freestanding ground support, which needs floor space and floor loading rather than anything from the ceiling. Flying the wall from the venue's load-rated rigging points changes the structure, the crew requirement and the venue conversation. Outdoors, ballast and wind loading join the calculation. Structure is part of the quote either way, but the method moves the figure.

We deliver and install LED walls throughout Ireland: Dublin, Cork, Galway, Belfast, Limerick and everywhere between. Distance affects trucking and crew time, and it's built into the all-inclusive figure rather than arriving later as a surcharge.

A one-day conference and a three-day exhibition use the same build, but not the same equipment days or crew hours. Multi-day events, rehearsal days and overnight holds all move the number, though usually by less than organisers fear, because the build and de-rig only happen once.

## Where to save money (and where not to)

**Save on area, carefully.** Size is the biggest lever at €125 per m². Size the wall from your furthest viewer, then stop: square metres nobody needs are the most expensive kind.

**Save on pitch.** If the front row sits three metres back, 2.6mm panels do the job, and paying for a finer pitch buys detail nobody can see. Size moves hire cost far more than pitch does.

**Save on schedule.** Flexible build windows and daytime de-rigs need fewer unsocial crew hours than midnight turnarounds. If the venue allows an easy get-in, say so. It helps the figure.

**Don't save on the operator.** An unwatched wall at a live event is a risk no saving justifies. A frozen slide in front of 300 delegates is a very public way to prove it.

**And check the alternative honestly.** If your room is genuinely dark, the audience small and the content slides, a projector may serve you fine. Tell us that's the room you have and we'll say so, rather than sell you a wall.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the €125 per m² rate the whole price?**
No. It's the dry hire rate for the equipment alone. An operated event adds crew, transport, structure, power distribution and show-day operation, quoted as one all-inclusive figure with no hidden fees.

**Why price per square metre rather than per panel?**
Panel sizes vary between manufacturers, so per-panel prices are hard to compare. Area is area: a per-m² rate can be held against any competing quote once you fix the size and pitch.

**Does pixel pitch change the hire price?**
Far less than size does. Specify the coarsest pitch your nearest viewer allows and spend the difference on the square metres the back row will actually notice.

**How do I compare two LED wall quotes fairly?**
Fix the size and pitch, then compare inclusions: build and show crew, operator hours, processing and spares, transport, power distribution, and who carries the de-rig. The lowest headline figure with the shortest inclusions list is rarely the cheapest event.

**When should I book to get the best value?**
Once dates and venue are firm. Autumn conference season and December concentrate demand, and build slots are easier to schedule (and price) with notice.

# 3. Video Walls for Theatre, Drama and School Shows: Digital Backdrops That Work

> **File:** `content/blog/video-walls-for-theatre-drama.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-08-24 · **Target:** `video wall hire theatre` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

A painted cloth gives you one scene for the whole run. A video wall gives you every scene in the show, changed in the time it takes to press a button. That is why video wall hire for theatre, drama groups and school shows has become one of the most common requests across our [musical theatre](/services/musical-theatre/) and [LED video wall](/services/led-video-walls/) work. This guide explains what a digital backdrop involves in practice: how big the wall needs to be, how it stands or hangs on stage, how it fits alongside your venue's existing rig, and what it costs. It's written for directors, producers and committee members, not AV engineers.

## Why productions are swapping painted cloths for digital backdrops

Traditional scenic cloths are hired per scene, shipped in tubes and need fly bars or a tab track to change. Many school halls and community venues have no fly system at all, which locks the production into one static backdrop. Every extra location in the script becomes a compromise.

A video wall removes that constraint. The forest, the ballroom, the street and the palace are files in a playlist, and moving between them is a cue rather than a scene change. Backdrops can also move: falling snow, drifting clouds, a slow sunset under the final number.

The honest trade-off is that a wall is not a set. Levels, furniture, trucks and props still do the physical work, and the strongest designs put real scenic pieces in front of a digital backdrop. What the wall replaces is the painted cloth at the back, along with the van, the crew and the interval needed to change it.

## How a digital backdrop works on stage

### Size: fill the frame the audience sees

The wall should fill the visual frame behind the acting area, not the entire stage. For most school halls and mid-size theatres, that means matching the width of the proscenium opening or masking, with the top edge comfortably above head height on any raised levels. Our walls build from 6m² upwards in modular panels, so the shape is made to fit the venue, including curved and angled configurations where the design calls for them.

> [TAILOR: a wide production photo showing the LED backdrop behind performers with the front rows of the audience visible. It makes the sizing and sightline points at a glance.]

### Ground support or flying

Panels either fly from rigging points or stand on a freestanding ground-support structure behind the acting area. Ground support is the usual answer in school halls and venues without certified rigging: it needs no fly tower, just a level floor and some upstage depth. If you'd rather fly the wall from house bars, the venue must confirm the rated load first. Never assume. We build both ways.

### Pixel pitch and your front row

Pixel pitch is the spacing between the LEDs, and it decides how close the audience can sit before the image breaks into visible dots. The working rule of thumb: minimum viewing distance in metres roughly equals the pixel pitch in millimetres. Theatre front rows typically sit three to four metres from the backdrop line, so the 2.6mm Absen panels we stock hold up well. Our range runs from 1.9mm to 3.9mm. What pitch and size do to the hire figure is broken down in [what LED wall hire costs in Ireland](/blog/led-wall-hire-cost-ireland/).

## Scene changes at the push of a button

Backdrops load as a numbered cue stack that mirrors the script: Act One, Scene Three is a cue, and it comes up when the operator takes it. Changes can snap on a blackout or crossfade gently under dialogue. That choice is a directing decision, made scene by scene.

The cue list gets plotted at the technical rehearsal, alongside lighting. Write the video cues into the same prompt book the stage manager already keeps, so one person calls the show and everything moves together.

## Working with your venue's house rig

Most venues a show lands in already have something. A lighting bar, a small PA, a projector past its best. We work with schools, musical societies and community theatre groups on a regular basis, and we integrate with existing house rigs in venues nationwide rather than duplicating kit that already works.

The adjustment that surprises first-time productions is brightness. An LED wall indoors runs well below full output. At full tilt it silhouettes the cast and swamps the lighting design. Balancing the wall against stage lighting is part of the technical rehearsal, not something to discover on opening night.

If radio mics and sound are on your list too, put both in the same enquiry. It's one crew and one technical rehearsal rather than two of each.

## Common mistakes with theatre video walls

1. **Booking the wall for show day only.** Cues need plotting with lighting and sound, so the wall should be in for the technical rehearsal, not arriving the afternoon of opening night.
2. **Content in the wrong shape.** Portrait phone images and old 4:3 artwork stretch badly across a wide backdrop. Source backdrops at, or near, the wall's aspect ratio.
3. **Ignoring the front row.** A pitch that looks fine from row K can pixelate for the most expensive seats. Check the nearest viewer, not the average one.
4. **Running the wall at full brightness.** It flattens faces and buries the lighting design. Trust the level set at tech.
5. **Assuming the bars can take the weight.** Flying panels needs confirmed load ratings from the venue. Ground support removes the question entirely.
6. **Treating the wall as the whole set.** It replaces the cloth, not the levels, furniture and props that give actors something to act on.

## Planning checklist

- Stage opening width and height, or hall dimensions if there is no proscenium
- Distance from the backdrop position to the front row
- Fly bars with confirmed rated loads, or floor space for ground support
- Power available on or near the stage
- Get-in access: door sizes, steps, lifts, parking
- Who holds the keys to the house rig, lighting and sound
- Where backdrop content is coming from, and at what resolution
- A cue list owner (usually the stage manager's prompt book)
- The technical rehearsal date the wall must be ready for

## Frequently asked questions

**Do we need a fly tower or rigging?**
No. A freestanding ground-support structure stands the wall behind the acting area, which is how most school hall and community venue builds go in.

**Where do the digital backdrops come from?**
Licensed scenery libraries sell show-specific digital backdrop packs for many popular musicals, and some productions commission their own stills or animations. We handle playback and cueing through the run.

**Can the wall do more than backdrops?**
Yes. Pre-show welcome screens, sponsor slides at the interval and a curtain-call montage are all common uses of the same wall on the same night.

**How far ahead should a school or society book?**
School show and panto season concentrates into November to January, so popular weeks go first. Book once your performance dates are confirmed with the venue.

# 4. Sound and Wireless Mics for School Musicals and Panto: A 30-Channel Survival Guide

> **File:** `content/blog/school-musical-sound-hire.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-08-27 · **Target:** `school musical sound hire` · **Tailoring spots:** 3

Forty cast members, a live band, a chorus that sings from the wings. And a hall built for assemblies, not theatre. Sound is the part of a school musical or panto that parents notice when it goes wrong and never notice when it goes right. Getting it right means school musical sound hire that covers four things: headset radio mics on the principals, coverage for everyone else, a PA the hall can't provide, and an operator who mixes every performance. This guide works through each, including how a 30-channel rig fills up faster than you'd expect. It draws on our [musical theatre](/services/musical-theatre/) work with schools, musical societies and community theatre groups across Ireland.

## What a school musical actually needs from sound

Strip away the kit list and a musical sets four audio jobs. Dialogue must be intelligible at the back row. Solo vocals must sit above a live band. Chorus numbers need lift without a mic on every child. And the cast needs to hear the band clearly enough to stay in time and in tune.

A hall's ceiling speakers were designed for announcements, not for that. We scale a proper PA to the room (line array for bigger, deeper venues, point source for smaller halls), with monitors on stage for foldback.

### Headset mics for the principals

Principal roles go on body-worn headset or lavalier radio mics, worn at the cheek or hairline and taped for choreography. Headsets beat handhelds for a musical because hands are busy and costumes change fast. Our wireless stock is Shure and Sennheiser, which matters less for the badge and more for how they behave in a crowded radio environment with thirty packs running.

### Covering the chorus without forty packs

Nobody needs a transmitter on every member of a fifty-strong chorus. No budget survives it anyway. Big ensemble numbers are lifted with a small number of area microphones across the front of the stage, mixed in only when the chorus sings. It's not as precise as a personal mic, and it's not meant to be. It reinforces a sound the chorus is already making together.

### The band, playback and foldback

The band eats channels quietly: DIs on keys and bass, mics on drums, brass and reeds, plus a line for backing tracks or click where the show uses them. Foldback matters just as much. Young casts drift flat when they can't hear the band, so stage monitors are part of the spec, not a luxury. If the band won't fit in front of the stage, a low platform sorts sightlines and cabling. That comes under our [staging and pipe and drape hire](/services/staging-pipe-drape/), so ask for it in the same quote.

## How 30 channels disappear: a worked example

Here is a realistic channel plan for a cast-of-forty school musical or a society panto.

| Source | Typical channels |
|---|---|
| Principal cast on headset mics | 12–16 |
| Swap packs for doubled roles and covers | 2–4 |
| Handhelds: MC, panto audience numbers, walk-ons | 2 |
| Area coverage for chorus scenes | 3–4 |
| Band: keys, bass, guitar, drums, brass, reeds | 6–10 |
| Playback and click | 1–2 |
| **Total** | **26–38** |

That total is why our wireless rigs support 30-plus simultaneous channels. It's also why a swap chart matters: when one actor plays three roles, the pack follows a written plan through the wings, not a whispered handover in the dark.

## The operator matters more than the mic count

Theatre mixing isn't "set it and forget it". Somebody rides fifteen open vocal channels cue by cue, mutes packs the moment actors exit, catches the missed pickup, and keeps the band under the dialogue. An unmuted mic in a toilet or a green room has ruined more school shows than any equipment failure.

Our quotes are all-inclusive (equipment, delivery, installation, on-site operation and breakdown), which for a show run means the engineer who plots your technical rehearsal is the one mixing every performance. The tech rehearsal is where mic swaps, scene-by-scene levels and problem moments get solved, so protect that date in your schedule.

## Panto season timing: book before the rush

Irish school musicals cluster into late November and December, with a second wave in spring, and panto runs from late November into January. Every group needs the same weekends, and rigs of this size aren't infinite. Rehearsals typically start in September. The right moment to book sound is the week your performance dates are confirmed with the venue.

If your show opens in November, the time to lock in kit and crew is now, not at half-term. Late bookings tend to compromise on dates for the technical rehearsal, which is exactly the day you cannot afford to lose.

## Working with what your hall already has

If your venue has a house PA, lighting bars or an installed desk, we integrate with existing house rigs rather than duplicating them. We do this in venues nationwide. Sometimes the house system earns its keep for foldback or fills. Sometimes it's best left for interval announcements. A quick conversation about what's installed, ideally with photos, sorts this before quote stage.

## Common mistakes with school show sound

1. **Hiring mics without an operator.** Thirty channels don't mix themselves, and a volunteer at an unfamiliar desk isn't a fair ask on show night.
2. **No swap plan for doubled roles.** Packs shared on the fly cause missed entrances and open-mic accidents. Write the swap chart into the prompt book.
3. **Skipping the technical rehearsal.** Levels, swaps and band balance get solved at tech. Without it, the first performance becomes the rehearsal.
4. **No foldback.** If the cast can't hear the band, pitch drifts and tempos wander, and no amount of front-of-house mixing fixes it.
5. **Relying on the hall's ceiling speakers.** They were specified for speech at assembly volume, not for vocals over a live band.
6. **Ignoring batteries and taping.** Fresh batteries each performance and properly taped headsets are boring and cheap. They're also the difference between a clean run and a crackly one.

# 5. LED Wall or Projector? How to Choose for Your Conference Venue

> **File:** `content/blog/led-wall-or-projector.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-08-31 · **Target:** `led wall vs projector` · **Tailoring spots:** 3

The honest answer to the LED wall vs projector question is that your room decides most of it. In a space you can properly darken, with a modest audience and a tight budget, a projector remains the sensible choice. And this guide will say so more than once. In a lit room, at scale, in daylight or on camera, an [LED video wall](/services/led-video-walls/) wins on every axis except the smallest invoices. What follows is the comparison in plain terms: how each technology behaves, a side-by-side table, when the projector genuinely is the right call, and what the choice looks like in a typical Irish conference venue.

## The short answer

Choose a **projector** when all three of these are true: the room can be properly darkened, the audience is small enough to sit close to a modest screen, and the budget wants the cheapest workable image.

Choose an **LED wall** when any of these apply: the room stays lit, daylight is involved, the event is filmed or streamed, the audience is large, or the screen is carrying branding and production content rather than just slides.

That's the decision in miniature. The rest of this guide is the reasoning.

## Emitted light versus borrowed light

A projector bounces light off a screen, which means every other light in the room lands on that same screen and washes the image out. Contrast dies first. Blacks turn grey, colours flatten, the picture fades into the wall behind it. This is why cinemas are dark.

An LED wall emits its own light. Every pixel is a tiny lamp. House lights up, daylight through the windows, camera lighting on the presenters: the image holds. Indoor walls run far brighter than projection needs its room to be, and outdoor panels brighter again, because they compete with the sky.

Everything in the comparison below flows from that one difference.

## LED wall vs projector: side by side

| | Projector and screen | LED video wall |
|---|---|---|
| How the image is made | Reflected off a screen | Emitted by the panels |
| Room light | Needs real darkness to look its best | Holds up under full house light and daylight |
| Scale | Practical at typical ballroom screen sizes | Modular, built to the size and shape the room needs |
| Space | Needs throw distance, or depth behind the screen for rear projection | Stands against the stage wall on ground support, or flies from rigging |
| Setup | Quick where the venue is already rigged | Panel-by-panel build. Allow a proper window |
| On camera | Prone to washing out and flickering in shots | Reads cleanly for filming and streaming |
| Mid-show risk | Lamp, alignment and focus issues | Modular. A faulty panel can be swapped |
| Cost shape | Cheapest way to serve a small, dark room | Scales with area. Better value as size and brightness demands grow |
| Best for | Dark rooms, tight budgets, smaller audiences | Lit rooms, large audiences, cameras, outdoor |

## When a projector is the right choice

Candour, as promised. A projector is the right call when:

- **The room genuinely goes dark.** Purpose-built auditoriums and lecture theatres with proper blackout give projection everything it needs. If your venue can kill the light, the biggest argument for LED disappears.
- **The budget is tight and the content is slides.** For a modest room, a screen and a decent projector cost less than any wall we'd build. And a readable deck is a readable deck. Spending the savings on better sound is usually the smarter production decision.
- **The audience is small and close.** Forty people in a seminar room don't need square metres of LED. They need a clear image at a sensible size.
- **The venue already owns decent kit.** Many conference venues have an installed projector and screen. If it's bright enough for the room as you'll actually light it, use it. We'd rather tell you that than hire you something you don't need.

> [TAILOR: if EventSound also supplies projectors, adjust this section to say so. If not, keep the "use the venue's installed kit" framing.]

One caveat about the dark room: darkness suits the screen, not the people. Delegates take notes, panellists need their faces lit, dinners need light on the tables. If your format needs the lights on, the projector's ideal conditions are already gone.

## When the LED wall earns its money

- **The lights stay up.** Conference days run lit for note-taking and energy. Awards dinners need the room warm and visible. An LED wall keeps a punchy image in exactly the conditions that ruin projection. At the Mayors Awards in Dublin, our LED wall carried the category content while designed lighting states kept the room alive around it.
- **Daylight is involved.** Atriums, glazed ballrooms, daytime launches and anything outdoor.
- **Cameras are involved.** Filmed and streamed events need presenters lit, and the wall holds its image in shot instead of forcing a choice between readable slides and readable faces.
- **The room is big.** Once a screen must be large to stay legible from the back, projection brightness struggles while LED simply adds panels. Size becomes a build question rather than a brightness one.
- **The screen is the set.** Branding, sponsor loops and full-stage backdrops ask more of a screen than a slide deck does. Modular sizing and custom shapes are the point of LED.

## The hotel ballroom test

Most Irish conference days happen in hotel function rooms, and they're the honest test case. A ballroom rarely offers true blackout: there are windows, the chandeliers stay partly up, and the organiser wants delegates alert rather than dozing in the dark. Projection in those conditions is a compromise from the first slide.

An evening event in the same room shifts the calculation. If dinner is followed by a ten-minute presentation and the lights can drop for it, the installed projector may be perfectly adequate. Say so and save the money. If the screen carries the night, the wall earns its keep: the Music Awards at the Hillgrove Hotel in Monaghan is exactly that format, and it ran on an LED screen we supplied.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a projector always cheaper?**
At seminar-room scale, usually. As screens get bigger and rooms brighter, high-output projection plus screen, rigging and crew narrows the gap, and LED's per-square-metre pricing starts to compare well.

**Can we just dim the lights for the projector?**
You can, and for a short presentation it works. Across a full conference day it fights the format: note-taking, cameras and audience energy all want the lights up.

**What about a large TV instead of either?**
For boardrooms and breakout spaces, individual screens from 43" to 98" are often the right answer. Simpler than projection, cheaper than a wall. Ask us which suits the room.

**Which is better for streaming and filming?**
LED, comfortably. The wall holds exposure alongside lit presenters, while projection forces the camera to choose between a readable screen and readable faces.

# 6. Conference AV Planning in Ireland: The Technical Brief to Build Before You Request Quotes

> **File:** `content/blog/conference-av-technical-brief.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-09-03 · **Target:** `conference av planning` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Conference AV planning usually goes wrong the same way: quotes are requested before anyone has written down what the event needs. Three suppliers guess at three different events, the prices come back impossible to compare, and the gaps (Q&A microphones, streaming bandwidth, the rehearsal slot) surface in the room on the day.

The fix is a technical brief: one page covering the venue, the room, the audience, the programme, the microphone count, the screens, the streaming decision, power and the schedule. This guide works through each section and ends with a copy-paste template to send with every quote request. It takes an hour to build.

## Why the brief comes before the quotes

An AV quote is an answer, and vague questions get padded answers. Priced on "sound and screens for about 200 people", one supplier carries contingency for whatever you might mean and another strips the spec to look cheap. A written brief pins the event down.

Send a complete brief to our [conference AV hire](/services/conference-av-hire/) team and the reply comes back specific (a specification, a crew plan, one figure) instead of a week of follow-up questions.

## The venue and the room

### Dimensions and ceiling height

Width, depth and ceiling height drive more of the AV specification than anything except audience size. Depth sets how big the screens must be for the back row. Ceiling height caps screen size, stage height and anything rigged. If the venue can't supply dimensions, pace the room out. Rough numbers beat blanks.

### Power

Ask the venue what power the room actually has: wall sockets, dedicated circuits, or three-phase. A modest sound-and-screens setup runs happily on ordinary sockets. Bigger rigs and streaming positions need that answered before the specification is final, not on the morning.

### Access and the load-in

How kit gets in decides how long the build takes. Loading dock or front steps? Lift or stairs? How far do vehicles park from the room? Access belongs in the brief because crew hours are priced from it.

### What the venue already has

Many venues carry something already: a house PA, a projector, sometimes installed lighting. List it, even roughly. We integrate with existing house rigs nationwide, and a brief saying "house PA exists, quality unknown" lets a supplier price integration instead of blind duplication.

## The audience: size, layout and access needs

Audience size drives PA coverage and screen size, and it decides how Q&A works. Forty people can share two roving microphones. Four hundred need a plan. State the number, even as a range.

Layout matters nearly as much. Theatre rows, cabaret rounds, classroom tables and boardroom squares each change sightlines and where screens and speakers stand. If it's undecided, say which options are in play.

Accessibility belongs here rather than as an afterthought: hearing assistance, captioning for the room or the stream, clear sightlines for interpreters. Put what you know in the brief so it gets specified and priced.

## The programme: the running order drives the kit list

A day of keynotes needs a different rig from a day of panels. List the session formats (keynote, panel, Q&A, awards, breakouts) and the kit list follows. Every panellist needs a microphone. An awards segment needs handhelds and walk-up music. Each breakout room needs its own screen and sound.

Two more programme items earn their place. Rehearsal slots: when speakers can stand on stage with their slides, the single best predictor of a smooth morning. And a named decision-maker, the person who calls it when the schedule slips. That's the discipline behind conference productions like the PRISM Immersive Technology Summit at the Mac Anna Theatre.

## Audio: settle the microphone count

Speech intelligibility is the one non-negotiable: if the room can't hear clearly, nothing else matters. The brief's job is a realistic microphone count, and it falls straight out of the programme:

| Audience Q&A | Two roving handhelds | With runners, whatever the room size |

## Video: screens everyone can read

State what the audience must read from the back row (slides, video, a live camera feed) and where the room's daylight comes from. Bright, high-windowed rooms are hostile to projection, which is usually what tips a room towards [an LED wall rather than a projector](/blog/led-wall-or-projector/).

For rooms deep enough that faces vanish, note whether you want IMAG (live camera close-ups of the speaker put up on screen).

Two content lines complete the section: the slide aspect ratio (16:9 unless someone says otherwise, and someone should say), and whether speakers get a confidence monitor, the screen at their feet that stops them turning their back on the audience.

## Streaming and recording: decide early

"Streaming: yes or no" is the single line with the biggest budget consequence, because yes adds cameras, an encoder, an operator and a dependency on the venue's internet. Deciding in the final week is how streams end up running on hope.

Even a modest conference deserves three lines here. Lighting: faces matter double on camera, so if the event is filmed or streamed, say so. # 7. Live Streaming an AGM or Town Hall: Cameras, Platforms and What Can Go Wrong

> **File:** `content/blog/agm-town-hall-live-streaming.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-09-07 · **Target:** `agm live streaming` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

An AGM is a one-take event. There's no second recording session if the stream fails during the chairperson's report, and the meeting itself has formal standing (motions, votes, minutes). A town hall carries a different pressure: leadership answering unscripted questions in front of the whole organisation. AGM live streaming is therefore less about elaborate production and more about three decisions made properly: how many cameras, which platform, and what happens when something fails.

This guide walks through all three. The camera and vision-mixing side of the job sits under our [video production](/services/video-production/) service. The platform, remote-attendee and streaming side sits under [virtual events](/services/virtual-events/).

## What makes an AGM different from an ordinary stream

- **It's on the record.** Votes and proceedings may need a complete, accurate recording, not just a live feed.
- **The audience is defined.** Members, shareholders or staff. That changes the platform decision, because access control suddenly matters.
- **Questions come from the floor.** The least controllable audio at any event, and the part remote viewers most need to hear.
- **Rules may apply.** If remote attendees are meant to count towards quorum or vote, check your constitution or governing rules before you brief any AV supplier. That's a governance question, and it shapes the whole technical setup.

Sometimes a board member joins the meeting remotely. Or an auditor, or a guest speaker. If they're taking part rather than just watching, that's a hybrid session, and it needs its own rehearsal and audio plan. Flag it early. Our [conference AV technical brief guide](/blog/conference-av-technical-brief/) has a line for how many remote presenters are joining.

## Cameras: how many you actually need

### One camera, honestly

For a small AGM with a single top table and slides, one well-positioned locked-off camera with good audio is genuinely enough. Anyone insisting every AGM needs a multi-camera shoot is selling you something. The picture will be static. But the audience is there for the content and the votes, not the cinematography.

### Two or three cameras, the working standard

Once the meeting runs past an hour (or has several speakers, or matters reputationally), two to three cameras earn their keep: a wide shot of the top table, a closer shot of whoever is speaking, and optionally a third covering the floor for questions. A vision mixer cuts between them live, which keeps a two-hour meeting watchable and means the recording needs no editing afterwards.

If the room itself is large, the same cameras can feed screens in the room (known as IMAG, or image magnification) so the back rows see faces rather than dots.

> [TAILOR: a real AGM or town-hall job (organisation type, venue, rough in-room versus online attendance split, and the camera count used).]

## Audio decides whether the stream is watchable

Streams die on audio far more often than on video. Viewers forgive a soft picture. They leave over a voice they can't hear. Three rules:

- Every voice that matters gets a microphone: top table, lectern, and roving handhelds for floor questions.
- The stream gets its own dedicated audio mix, never a microphone pointed at the room PA.
- The chair enforces one discipline: no microphone, no question. Repeating the question from the chair's mic is the fallback, not the plan.

> [TAILOR: photo of the vision-mix and encoder position at the back of a meeting room, with specific alt text.]

## Choosing the platform

### YouTube or Vimeo: reach without friction

No logins, works on every device, no audience-size worries. An unlisted link narrows the audience, but it isn't security. Anyone holding the link can watch and share it. The right choice for public town halls and bodies content to be watched by anyone.

### Teams or Zoom: control and interaction

Registration, attendee lists, Q&A tools and, on some tiers, native polling. The trade-offs are join friction and attendee limits. The right choice when remote attendees must be identified, speak or vote.

### Your own website or member portal: custom RTMP

The stream can be pushed straight into a page you control, behind a member login if needed. The right choice for member-only AGMs where privacy matters but Teams-scale interaction is unnecessary.

We stream to YouTube, Vimeo, Teams, Zoom or a custom RTMP destination through broadcast-grade encoders, so the platform decision can follow your governance needs rather than an equipment limitation.

## The internet connection: where AGM streams actually fail

Venue wifi isn't a streaming connection. It's shared, variable and unmonitored, and it degrades at the exact moment the room fills with phones. A stream needs three things:

- **A dedicated wired connection.** We recommend a minimum 10Mbps dedicated upload (our working figure as of August 2026), with "dedicated" meaning reserved for the stream, not shared with the venue's guests.
- **A backup path.** We run redundant internet: the wired line backed by bonded cellular, which combines several mobile networks into one connection. If the venue line drops, the stream carries on.
- **A test in advance.** Measure the upload at the venue, ideally at the same time of day as the meeting, not on a quiet mid-morning.

> [TAILOR: a real venue-internet story, a venue whose broadband tested fine earlier in the day and struggled once the room filled, and what the backup did.]

## What can go wrong, and what prevents it

| Failure | What viewers experience | What prevents it |
|---|---|---|
| Dead or late link | "Starting soon" forever | Publish the link early. Test end-to-end the day before. Open with a holding slide |
| Inaudible floor questions | Half the meeting missing | Roving microphones and a chair who enforces them |
| Echo or hum | Fatigue and drop-off | A dedicated stream mix, not a mic at the PA |
| Venue broadband drops | Frozen picture mid-vote | Wired connection plus bonded-cellular backup |
| Confidential moment streamed | A governance problem | A pause protocol agreed between chair and operator |
| Recording lost | No record to support minutes | Record locally alongside the platform |

The table above is the AGM-specific slice of a longer discipline. Name the likely faults in advance, then remove each one with a test, a spare or a person in the room.

## A pre-stream checklist

- Governing rules checked for remote participation and voting, before the AV brief
- Platform chosen to match who may watch and who must interact
- Stream link published early and tested end-to-end
- Dedicated upload measured at the venue (10Mbps minimum)
- Backup connection in place
- Microphone plan covers the floor, not just the top table
- Dedicated stream mix confirmed
- Local recording running alongside the stream
- Pause protocol agreed between the chair and the operator
- One person assigned to watch the public stream as a viewer throughout

## Frequently asked questions

**Do we need to tell attendees the meeting is being streamed?**
Yes, in advance and on the day. And position cameras to favour the top table rather than the audience. If attendees will be identifiable on the stream, take data-protection advice appropriate to your organisation.

**Can remote members vote during the stream?**
Teams and Zoom offer polling on some tiers, and dedicated AGM voting services exist. Whether a remote vote counts is a rules question for your organisation, not a technical one. Settle it first.

**Can we stream and record at the same time?**
Yes, and you should. A local recording alongside the platform's copy protects the formal record and supports the minutes.

**How much notice does a supplier need?**
Enough to test the venue's connectivity and fix what the test finds. A few weeks is comfortable. Two days is a gamble.

# 8. What Actually Drives Conference AV Costs: Equipment, Crew, Rehearsals and Venue Constraints

> **File:** `content/blog/conference-av-costs-explained.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-09-10 · **Target:** `conference av costs` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Ask what conference AV costs and an honest supplier will answer with questions. How many rooms, what format, is it streamed, when do we get access? That's not evasion. The price is genuinely built out of those answers. Conference AV costs are driven by five things: the equipment the format demands, the crew who run it, the time on site, the constraints of the venue, and the contingency that keeps a live event live. As of August 2026, a straightforward single-room setup with sound and screens starts from around €1,500. A multi-room, streamed programme with rehearsals is a different project with a different number. This guide breaks down where the money goes, so the quotes you gather for [conference AV hire](/services/conference-av-hire/) make sense. And so the cheapest one gets the scrutiny it deserves.

## Why two quotes for the same conference can be thousands apart

Send the same loose enquiry ("AV for 200 people, 14 October") to three suppliers and the quotes won't match, because each fills the gaps with different assumptions. One assumes you'll operate the kit yourself. One prices a technician for the day. One adds a rehearsal, spare microphones and backup playback.

None of the three is lying. They're quoting different events. The spread between them is rarely padding. It's scope. So the first job in judging conference AV costs is understanding the five drivers below, and lining quotes up on identical scope matters more than the headline figures. The way to force that is one written brief sent to every supplier. Our [conference AV technical brief guide](/blog/conference-av-technical-brief/) has the template.

## Cost driver 1: equipment (rooms, screens and channels)

Equipment cost scales with rooms and format more than with headcount. Every additional room duplicates a sound system, a display and usually an operator. Two breakout rooms can add more to the bill than fifty extra delegates in the main room would.

Displays are the biggest single fork. Screens up to 98" cover breakouts and smaller plenary rooms. A modular LED wall transforms a main stage, and the budget with it, because a wall brings panels, build time and a technician of its own. Neither is wrong. They're different products for different rooms.

Audio scales with the format too. One presenter at a lectern is a modest brief. A day of panel discussions needs several radio channels live at once (headsets, lavaliers, handhelds for audience questions), plus the desk and the engineer to mix them. Our Shure and Sennheiser wireless rigs support 30+ simultaneous channels. Most conferences use far fewer, but every live channel means kit, frequency coordination and mixing attention.

Streaming is effectively a second production: cameras, vision mixing, a broadcast-grade encoder, dedicated connectivity and the crew to run it. It's the most common reason a conference AV quote jumps sharply. Reasonably enough, because the audience has just doubled. Remote presenters add their own workflow again. Our [guide to streaming an AGM or town hall](/blog/agm-town-hall-live-streaming/) walks through the camera, platform and connectivity side of it.

## Cost driver 2: crew, the line that should never be zero

A conference is live and one-take. The kit doesn't run itself. Someone mixes the panel so the softly spoken chair stays audible, cues the walk-in music, switches the screens between slides and speakers, and deals with the fault before the audience notices there was one.

Crew cost is a function of rooms and hours, then complexity on top of that. It isn't vanity. A simple single-room day might be one engineer. A streamed two-room programme needs sound, vision and camera roles filled separately. EventSound is owner-led, and on larger conferences Ronan and Mark make up the wider crew.

Remember the hours around the show. An event that runs 9am to 5pm isn't an eight-hour crew day once load-in, build, testing and de-rig are counted. When one quote is dramatically cheaper, crew is the first place to look: "equipment only" is a legitimate service, but it should be labelled dry hire, not discovered on the morning.

## Cost driver 3: rehearsals and time on site

Access time is invisible on a quote and decisive on the day. A room released at 6am for 9am doors means a compressed, riskier build. The same room available the previous evening means a tested rig and a calm morning. That difference appears in the price as crew hours or venue access, and it's usually money well spent.

A technical rehearsal is the same trade. Presentations opened on the show machine, microphones on real voices, the remote speaker dialled in, the awards video played at level. Problems surface while they're still free to fix. A quote that includes rehearsal time will look dearer than one that doesn't. It's usually the better buy, because it buys you the version of show morning where nothing surprises anyone.

## Cost driver 4: the venue

Two identical events in two different venues don't cost the same. The constraints that move the number:

- **Access.** A loading dock beside the ballroom is one job. A goods lift, three corridors and a 6am slot is another. Load-in distance converts directly into crew hours.
- **Rigging.** Whether screens and lighting can hang, or must be ground-supported, changes both the kit list and the build time. LED walls especially.
- **Power.** The rig needs adequate supply where the stage actually is. Finding the gap on show morning is the expensive way to learn the room.
- **The room itself.** Low ceilings cap screen size, pillars force repeater screens, and a glass wall behind the stage fights every camera.
- **Acoustics.** Reverberant halls need more careful PA choice and placement to keep speech intelligible at the back.

This is why we walk the venue before finalising a specification (site visits are free for confirmed bookings). An hour in the room removes most of the assumptions that would otherwise hide inside a price.

## Cost driver 5: contingency, the first line the cheap quote drops

Redundancy looks like the easiest saving, because most days nothing fails. Then a radio microphone dies mid-keynote in front of three hundred delegates.

Sensible conference contingency means spare radio microphones rigged and ready, backup equipment for the systems the event can't run without, and, for anything streamed, redundant internet. That means a wired line plus bonded cellular, because venue wifi is not a broadcast plan. For any livestream we recommend a minimum 10Mbps dedicated upload.

Contingency costs money to carry and is invisible when it works, which is exactly why the cheapest quote rarely carries it. Ask every supplier the same question: what happens, specifically, when a microphone fails at 10am?

# 9. The Conference AV Failure Checklist: What We Eliminate Before Doors Open

> **File:** `content/blog/conference-av-failure-checklist.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-09-14 · **Target:** `av checklist conference` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Conference AV rarely fails in exotic ways. It fails in the same handful of places, event after event: a microphone, a presentation, a signal path, an internet connection, a socket, a running order that changed after the last email. That predictability is the good news. A failure you can name in advance is a failure you can eliminate in advance. This is the thinking behind our [conference AV hire](/services/conference-av-hire/) and the wider [event production](/services/event-production/) work it sits inside. Name every likely fault, then remove each one before doors open with a test, a spare or a person.

Use this two ways. As a working AV checklist for your conference, and as a set of questions that reveals whether the supplier you're about to book runs one of their own.

## The six places conference AV fails

Twenty-plus years of show mornings reduce the list to six clusters.

### 1. Microphones

The classic failure is audio. A radio microphone dying mid-sentence, two channels clashing on the same frequency, a lavalier rustling under a lanyard. An audience forgives a slide glitch. It won't forgive ten minutes of inaudible keynote.

What eliminates it: spare radio microphones rigged and ready at the side of the stage, with a technician in the room who can swap a faulty unit in minutes. Usually between speakers, sometimes mid-panel. Frequency coordination and battery discipline at setup shrink the odds. The spares remove the consequence. Headroom matters too. Our Shure and Sennheiser rigs support 30+ simultaneous channels, so a surprise panellist is a patch, not a crisis.

> [TAILOR: a real save — an event where a mic was swapped live, and how quickly]

### 2. Presentations and playback

Every technician can recite the greatest hits: the video missing its codec, fonts reflowing on a different laptop, a 4:3 deck on a 16:9 screen, embedded audio nobody routed to the PA.

What eliminates it: every presentation tested on the actual show machine before the programme starts. Not glanced at. Opened, stepped through, videos played at level. Critical playback carries backup equipment, so the machine itself is never a single point of failure. The organiser's half of the bargain is collecting decks the day before. Day-of USB surprises are where this discipline goes to die.

### 3. Signal paths and screens

Between a laptop and a screen sit cables, adapters, switchers and scalers, and any one of them can take the stage dark mid-session.

What eliminates it: the full path tested end to end at build, every input and every screen, with backups on the links the show can't lose. One controlled playback position beats a relay of laptop handovers at the lectern every time.

### 4. Streaming and remote speakers

Hybrid is where small faults become public ones: the stream that buffers at the sponsor's segment, the remote keynote who can't connect at 8.50am.

What eliminates it: dedicated connectivity, never venue guest wifi. We run redundant internet (a wired line plus bonded cellular backup) and recommend a minimum 10Mbps dedicated upload for any livestream. Remote presenters get a test call in advance on the platform the event will actually use. Our [guide to streaming an AGM or town hall](/blog/agm-town-hall-live-streaming/) goes through the connection and platform decisions in detail.

### 5. Power

The unglamorous one. A rig sharing a circuit with the catering urns, a breaker tripping mid-morning, a vital socket switched off by a wall panel nobody noticed.

What eliminates it: power mapped before show day, with loads spread across known circuits and the sockets behind the stage treated as production infrastructure, not conveniences. (Site visits are free for confirmed bookings.)

### 6. The programme itself

The last failure point has nothing to do with hardware. It's the gap between the printed programme and what actually happens: the surprise video, the extra panellist without a microphone, the award category nobody mentioned to the desk.

What eliminates it: the AV team holding the current run of show, channel and input headroom built into the rig, and one person on the organiser's side empowered to feed changes to the desk as they happen.

# 10. Pixel Pitch Without the Jargon: Specifying an LED Wall by Viewing Distance

> **File:** `content/blog/pixel-pitch-explained.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-09-17 · **Target:** `pixel pitch explained` · **Tailoring spots:** 3

Here's the rule of thumb that settles most pixel pitch questions: the minimum comfortable viewing distance in metres roughly equals the pixel pitch in millimetres. A 2.6mm wall looks solid from about 2.6 metres. A 3.9mm wall wants closer to four. If your nearest viewer stands further back than that, they can't see individual pixels, and paying for a finer pitch buys detail nobody in the room can perceive. This guide is pixel pitch explained without the jargon: what the number actually measures, where the rule of thumb bends, what pitch does to resolution and cost, and how to specify an [LED video wall](/services/led-video-walls/) from your room rather than from a spec sheet.

## What pixel pitch actually is

An LED wall is a grid of tiny lights, and pixel pitch is simply the distance from the centre of one pixel to the centre of the next, measured in millimetres. A 2.6mm pitch means the pixels sit 2.6mm apart. Smaller number, tighter grid, more detail packed into every square metre.

The useful comparison is print. A magazine page and a motorway billboard can carry the same photograph. The billboard just uses far bigger dots, because nobody reads it from half a metre away. LED pitch works on exactly the same logic. The further away your audience is, the coarser the grid can be before anyone notices.

That's the whole concept. Everything else in an LED wall specification (resolution, cost, content requirements) follows from that one distance-versus-detail trade.

## The viewing-distance rule of thumb, and where it bends

The industry shorthand: **minimum viewing distance in metres ≈ pixel pitch in millimetres.** Applied to the pitches we stock:

| Pixel pitch | Rule-of-thumb minimum viewing distance | Where it typically fits |
|---|---|---|
| 1.9mm | ~2m | Exhibition stands, small rooms, anywhere people walk right up to the screen |
| 2.6mm | ~2.5–3m | Conferences, awards nights, stage and theatre backdrops (the workhorse pitch) |
| 3.9mm | ~4m | Bigger rooms and outdoor stages where the audience starts further back |

Coarser pitches than these exist for large outdoor sites viewed from far away. But 1.9mm to 3.9mm covers the events we build for.

Be clear about what this rule is: a field-tested approximation, not a law of optics. It marks the distance where individual pixels stop being obvious to a typical viewer, not the distance where the image is at its absolute finest. Some integrators quote a stricter figure of two to three times the pitch for "critical" viewing of fine text and detailed graphics. Eyesight, brightness and content all shift the line a little.

The practical reading: treat metres-equals-millimetres as the floor. If your content is mostly video and imagery, the floor is fine. If people will read dense text or data up close, add margin by going a step finer or moving the front row back.

## What pitch does to resolution: the part people miss

Pitch does more than set how close people can stand. It fixes how many pixels your wall has, and that decides how your content behaves.

The maths is friendly. One metre is 1,000mm, so divide 1,000 by the pitch to get pixels per metre of wall:

- 1.9mm → ~526 pixels per metre
- 2.6mm → ~384 pixels per metre
- 3.9mm → ~256 pixels per metre

Now apply it to a typical conference backdrop of 5m × 3m. At 2.6mm that wall is roughly 1,920 × 1,150 pixels, effectively a full-HD canvas. The same wall at 3.9mm is about 1,280 × 770 pixels. Both look excellent from the seats. The difference only appears in small text, fine logos and hairline graphics.

This is why pitch and content have to be specified together. Ask your supplier for the pixel canvas before anyone opens PowerPoint. Slides built for the wrong canvas either get scaled and softened or arrive with text sized for a laptop screen, not a stage.

## Specify from the room, not the spec sheet

The reliable sequence runs in four steps, and pitch is deliberately step three.

**1. Find your nearest viewer.** Front row, stage edge, or the walkway beside an exhibition stand. Measure to where the closest pair of eyes will actually be, not to the middle of the room.

**2. Size the wall from the furthest viewer and the content.** The back row decides how big the image must be to stay legible. The room's sightlines and ceiling height cap it. Size, not pitch, is what makes a wall feel impressive from the cheap seats.

**3. Choose the coarsest pitch your nearest viewer allows.** If nobody gets within four metres, 3.9mm is the honest choice. Front row at three metres, take 2.6mm. People touching-distance away, 1.9mm.

**4. Sanity-check the resolution.** Divide the wall width by the pitch and make sure the pixel canvas suits the content, especially if there's dense text, scoreboard data or a broadcast camera involved.

Our stock is Absen P2.6 and Unilumin panels from 1.9mm to 3.9mm, built from 6m² up beyond 50m². Across that range, the sequence answers almost every event brief. The 2.6mm panels earn their reputation as the default because typical front rows sit right around that three-metre line.

## What this means for common event types

### Conferences and awards nights

Front rows and top tables usually sit three to five metres from the stage, which is comfortably inside 2.6mm territory. If the event is being filmed or streamed, a finer pitch also behaves better on camera. Coarse grids can shimmer (moiré) in tight shots, so tell your supplier if IMAG or broadcast is part of the brief.

### Theatre and stage backdrops

Backdrops sit upstage of the performers, which buys extra distance from the audience. But check the actual front-row-to-wall measurement rather than guessing. Panto and school-show sightlines are often closer than organisers expect.

### Outdoor stages and festivals

Outdoor audiences start much further back, so pitch relaxes and 3.9mm does the work. Brightness and weather-rating become the critical specs instead. Our outdoor panels are weather-rated, and outdoor walls run far brighter than indoor ones to beat daylight.

### Exhibition stands and foyers

This is 1.9mm country. Passers-by stop within arm's reach, read text at conversational distance, and photograph the stand for LinkedIn. It's the one setting where the finest pitch routinely pays for itself.

## Common mistakes when specifying pixel pitch

1. **Choosing pitch before size.** Size comes from the room and the back row. Pitch comes from the front row. Doing it backwards produces small, over-specified walls.
2. **Buying the finest pitch "to be safe".** Past the viewing-distance floor the upgrade is invisible. Safe is matching the pitch to the measured nearest viewer.
3. **Measuring to the middle of the room.** The front row, the lectern and the VIP table are the viewers that matter for pitch.
4. **Ignoring the content resolution.** A 4K file doesn't make a 1,280-pixel wall a 4K wall. Build content for the canvas the wall actually has.
5. **Assuming indoor specs work outdoors.** Outdoor walls need weather-rated panels and several times the brightness. Pitch is the least of the changes.
6. **Comparing quotes on pitch alone.** Crew, processing, rigging and what's included vary between suppliers far more than the panels do.

No. Below your nearest viewer's distance threshold the improvement is invisible, and on a fixed budget extra square metres beat extra pixel density.

**Does pixel pitch matter for filming and streaming?**
Yes. Coarser grids can produce moiré shimmer in tight camera shots, so flag IMAG or broadcast early and test framing at rehearsal.

**What about outdoor events?**
Audiences stand further back, so 3.9mm typically does the job. The specs to interrogate outdoors are brightness and weather-rating.

# 11. How to Prepare Presentation Content for a Big LED Wall (Aspect Ratios, Safe Areas, Playback)

> **File:** `content/blog/led-wall-presentation-content.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-09-21 · **Target:** `led wall presentation content` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Most presentation problems on show day were baked in weeks earlier, when someone built a deck for a laptop screen instead of the wall it would actually play on. LED walls are modular. They're built to whatever size and shape the event needs, and that shape is often not 16:9. So the result is familiar: stretched logos, cropped titles, text hidden behind a lectern.

This guide covers how to prepare LED wall presentation content properly: confirming the canvas, setting up slides, respecting safe areas, exporting video that behaves and testing playback before doors. The advice applies whichever supplier builds your screen. EventSound builds [LED video walls](/services/led-video-walls/) from 6m² to 50m²+, including curved and custom-shaped configurations. That's exactly where the standard 16:9 assumption falls apart.

## Start with the canvas, not the deck

An LED wall is assembled from panels, so its shape follows the design of the event rather than a television standard. A 4m × 2.25m wall happens to be 16:9. A 6m × 2.5m wall behind a conference stage is far wider, and a curved or column-shaped build bears no resemblance to a slide at all.

Before anyone designs anything, get two numbers from your AV supplier:

1. **The content resolution in pixels.** That's the canvas your content should be built to. For example, a 4m × 2m wall of 2.6mm panels is roughly 1,536 × 768 pixels.
2. **The aspect ratio.** The shape of that canvas, plus any areas the set design will cover.

LED walls carry fewer pixels than people expect. A wall several metres wide can have a lower resolution than the laptop it's designed on, and that's fine. It's viewed from metres away, not centimetres. What matters is designing to the real canvas. Our guide to [pixel pitch and viewing distance](/blog/pixel-pitch-explained/) explains why resolution and sharpness depend on where the audience sits.

At the Beta Festival in The Digital Hub, Dublin, we built a curved LED wall for an art and technology exhibition. A canvas like that can't simply take a PowerPoint export. The content has to be made for the shape.

> [TAILOR: the Beta Festival curved wall. Actual dimensions and shape, a photo, and how the exhibition content was mapped onto it.]

## Set the slide size before you design anything

Every major tool can match a custom canvas, but the setting is buried and the order matters.

- **PowerPoint:** Design → Slide Size → Custom Slide Size. PowerPoint measures slides in centimetres rather than pixels, so match the ratio: for a 2:1 canvas, 50.8cm × 25.4cm works perfectly.
- **Keynote:** use a custom slide size, which can be set in pixels directly.
- **Google Slides:** File → Page setup → Custom.

Set the size first, then design. Resizing an existing deck reflows every layout, and "we'll tidy it later" turns into an hour of nudging text boxes the night before the event.

If the deck already exists and can't be rebuilt, the honest fallback is pillarboxing: the deck plays at its correct ratio and the remaining wall width carries a background or branding. A pillarboxed deck looks deliberately framed. A stretched one looks wrong to every person in the room.

## Respect the safe areas

On stage, parts of the wall are routinely obstructed. The bottom edge sits behind the lectern, panellists' heads or set pieces, and on very wide walls the outer edges fall outside comfortable reading range for people seated at the sides.

Practical rules that survive contact with a real stage:

- Keep titles, figures and anything that must be read inside the central 90% of the canvas (roughly a 5% margin on every side).
- Keep the bottom 10–15% free of critical content if a lectern or seated panel sits in front of the wall.
- On wide canvases, centre-weight the message and use the outer thirds for imagery or brand texture.
- Check lower-third name straps against the stage plan, not against a blank slide.

> [TAILOR: an annotated photo of a real EventSound conference wall showing where the lectern and stage furniture overlapped the canvas.]

## Design for the back of the room

A slide that reads perfectly at arm's length can be illegible from row twenty. Design for the furthest paying seat.

- Use fewer words and larger type than feels natural on a laptop. A quick test: view the slide on your laptop from three metres away. That's roughly a mid-room seat's experience.
- Prefer light text on dark backgrounds. LED is an emissive, bright medium: large pure-white slides can dominate a dim room and leave presenters silhouetted in front of a glowing rectangle.
- Avoid hairline table borders, 1px rules and fine serif detail. On coarser pixel pitches there are simply not enough pixels to draw them cleanly.
- Be careful with highly saturated single-colour text, especially pure red. It tends to look soft on LED. Slightly desaturated colours read cleaner.

## Video: formats that behave on show day

Video causes more show-day drama than slides, and almost all of it is avoidable at export time.

- Supply each video as a separate file as well as embedded in the deck. An MP4 (H.264) at 1080p or the canvas resolution is the safe default.
- Use a constant frame rate. Screen recordings from phones and Teams calls are often variable frame rate, which plays fine on a laptop and stutters through a playback system. Re-export at a constant 25 or 50 fps, which also matches camera feeds if your session uses IMAG.
- For 1080p playback, a bitrate around 20–30 Mbps is comfortable. Extreme bitrates gain nothing and can choke playback machines.
- Embed audio at 48kHz stereo and tell the AV team which videos carry sound, so it's routed and checked rather than discovered.
- If content plays through a media server, the operator may request ProRes or HAP versions. Ask before exporting. It saves a re-render.
- Name files so a stranger can run the show: `03_keynote_smith_v2.mp4` beats `final_FINAL_new.mp4` every time.

## Playback: how your content actually reaches the wall

Nobody plugs a laptop straight into an LED wall. Content goes to a playback machine, then through an LED processor that maps the image onto the panels at the agreed resolution. That has three practical consequences.

**Present from the show machine where possible.** A presenter's own laptop brings adapter roulette, notifications, sleep mode and missing fonts to the stage. If someone genuinely must present from their own device (a live software demo, say), flag it in advance so resolution, adapters and audio can be planned.

**Embed your fonts.** In PowerPoint: File → Options → Save → embed fonts in the file. Otherwise the playback machine substitutes its own and your careful layout reflows. A PDF copy of the deck is a useful belt-and-braces backup: animations are lost, but the layout is bulletproof.

**Presenters never need to turn around.** A confidence monitor at the foot of the stage shows the current slide, and a clicker advances the deck. Ask your AV team to confirm both. A presenter craning at the wall behind them reads as unprepared, and it's entirely avoidable.

## Test before show day, not at ten to nine

Every deck should be tested on the actual playback system: fonts, aspect ratio, animations, embedded video and audio routing. At our own events, presentations are tested before the programme starts. The deadline for content exists so that testing can actually happen.

Agree a handover deadline with your AV supplier a few working days out. Late changes on the morning are a fact of conference life, but they should be edits to a tested deck, not the first time anyone has seen the file. For anything business-critical (a launch film, an awards sting), ask for a full run in rehearsal.

## The handover checklist

- Canvas confirmed in pixels, with aspect ratio and any obstructed zones
- Slide size set to match **before** design work started
- Critical content inside the safe area, bottom margin respected
- Fonts embedded, plus a PDF backup of the deck
- Videos supplied as separate MP4s, constant frame rate, audio flagged
- Files named with running order, presenter and version
- Everything delivered by the agreed deadline
- Own-laptop demos flagged in advance
- Deck tested on the show system before the programme starts

## Frequently asked questions

**What resolution should slides be for an LED wall?**
Ask your supplier for the content resolution and build to that. The aspect ratio matters more than the pixel count. A correctly shaped deck scales cleanly. A wrongly shaped one never looks right.

**Can we just use our normal 16:9 deck?**
If the wall is 16:9, yes. If it's wider, either rebuild the key slides at the correct ratio or pillarbox the deck against a designed background. Never stretch it.

**Why does our video stutter on the big screen?**
The usual culprits are variable frame rate recordings, extreme bitrates or playing from a cloud link. Supply a local, constant-frame-rate MP4 and test it on the show machine.

**When should we send our content?**
By whatever deadline your AV team sets. That's typically a few working days before the event, so every file is tested before the programme starts rather than during it.

# 12. Hybrid Speakers Without the Awkwardness: A Production Workflow for Remote Presenters

> **File:** `content/blog/hybrid-remote-speaker-workflow.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-09-24 · **Target:** `remote speakers conference` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Everyone has watched it happen. The remote keynote appears on the big screen, asks "can you hear me?", hears their own voice come back a second late, and stumbles. Remote speakers at a conference fail for predictable reasons, and every one of them is preventable. Not by luck. By running a production workflow instead of balancing a laptop on the lectern.

This guide sets out that workflow: a connection test, a rehearsal call, return video so the presenter can see the room, mix-minus audio so they never hear an echo, a tested backup and a named operator. It applies to a single remote panellist just as much as a headline keynote. If you're planning a fully hybrid or streamed event, our [virtual events](/services/virtual-events/) page covers the wider production setup.

## Why remote speakers go wrong

The room and the platform are two different sound and picture worlds. The room has a PA system, microphones, screens and an audience. The platform has one webcam, one microphone and whatever the presenter's broadband is doing that afternoon. Connecting the two properly is an integration job, and when nobody owns that job, the join is exactly where things break.

The classic failures are worth naming, because the whole workflow exists to remove them:

- **Echo.** The speaker hears their own voice coming back from the room, delayed, and can't form a sentence.
- **Talking over people.** They can't hear the room clearly, miss the chair's cues and interrupt.
- **The frozen face.** A connection drops with no fallback, and the audience watches a pixelated still frame while someone fiddles with a laptop.
- **Presenting into the void.** With no view of the audience, the speaker's energy dies inside two minutes.

> [TAILOR: a real hybrid session EventSound produced — the event, number of remote contributors, platform used, and the one thing that nearly went wrong.]

## The workflow, step by step

### 1. A connection test the week before

Every remote presenter gets a short test call on the platform the event will actually use, not whichever one they prefer. The test covers upload stability, camera framing, lighting, background noise and, most importantly, the microphone. An inexpensive wired headset beats a laptop microphone in an echoey home office every time.

The test call is also where practical details get locked in: the room they will present from, wired ethernet rather than wifi where possible, and two phone numbers in case everything else fails.

### 2. A rehearsal call on the real system

The tech check proves the connection works. The rehearsal proves the session works. It covers the handovers: who introduces the speaker, who advances the slides and on what cue, how audience questions will reach them, what the timing signals are, and what happens if they drop. The best rehearsal happens on show morning through the actual room system, so the presenter experiences the real return feed and real audio rather than a simulation.

### 3. Return video: let the speaker see the room

In-room presenters get a confidence monitor. Remote presenters need the equivalent: a return video feed showing the stage or audience and, ideally, the programme output (what the audience is actually seeing at that moment).

With return video, the speaker sees the MC welcome them, knows when their slides are live, reads the room's reaction and can pause for a laugh instead of ploughing through it. Without it, they're performing into a black hole, and it shows.

> [TAILOR: photo — a remote presenter's return-video view, or the hybrid operator position at a real event, with specific alt text.]

### 4. Mix-minus audio, in plain English

The awkward echo has one specific cause. If you send the presenter the full room mix, that mix includes their own voice, delayed by the round trip. Hearing yourself half a second late is genuinely disorientating. Nobody can speak fluently through it.

The fix is called mix-minus. The presenter receives everything from the room (the chair, the other panellists, audience questions) minus their own voice. Every competent hybrid setup runs one. If you only ask a prospective AV supplier one technical question, ask how they handle mix-minus for remote contributors. The answer tells you a lot.

### 5. A backup that has already been tested

Broadband rarely fails, but "rarely" isn't a plan. A workable backup chain looks like this. A second device on a different network (typically a phone on 4G or 5G), already signed into the call and muted. Then a plain telephone dial-in as the last resort, so the session can continue audio-only with slides driven from the room.

The important part isn't the kit. It's that the backup was tested during rehearsal, and that everyone agreed in advance who decides to switch, and when.

> [TAILOR: a real save — a session where the backup device or dial-in was actually used, and how long the switch took.]

## What the operator does during the session

A hybrid session needs a named operator whose only job is the remote link. During the session they:

- monitor the presenter's connection and switch to the backup at the first sign of sustained degradation, not after the freeze
- ride the remote audio level so the presenter sits at the same loudness through the PA as an in-room voice
- mute and unmute at the handovers, so coughs and keyboard noise never reach the room
- manage the return feed and stay on talkback with whoever is calling the show
- watch the platform chat, which is where a presenter quietly flags a problem

"Set it and forget it" is how you get the frozen face. At EventSound we run hybrid links on Teams, Zoom, YouTube, Vimeo or a custom RTMP destination through broadcast-grade encoders, with redundant internet (a wired line backed by bonded cellular), so the remote link never hangs off venue wifi.

## What this means for your run of show

Remote segments need margin. Don't sandwich a remote keynote between two tight in-room items with zero slack. Practical adjustments cost nothing:

- allow a short buffer either side of each remote segment
- group remote contributions where the format allows, so there are fewer handovers
- brief the chair or MC with a holding script in case a reconnection takes a minute
- decide in advance whether a dropped speaker rejoins immediately, at the next break, or not at all

This is the same discipline that protects the rest of the day. See [the conference AV failure checklist](/blog/conference-av-failure-checklist/) for what a production team eliminates before doors open.

## A hybrid speaker checklist

- Platform confirmed and consistent with the production plan
- Connection test completed on that platform, headset in use
- Wired internet at the presenter's end where possible
- Two phone numbers collected and a dial-in fallback tested
- Rehearsal call covering handovers, slides, Q&A and the drop plan
- Return video agreed and tested
- Mix-minus confirmed with the AV supplier
- Named operator for the session
- Buffer time in the run of show around each remote segment
- Chair briefed with a holding script

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a remote speaker take live questions from the audience?**
Yes, provided every question reaches a microphone, either a roving mic or a chair who repeats the question. The mix-minus feed carries the question to the presenter, and return video lets them see who's asking.

**What internet speed does the presenter need?**
Stability matters more than headline speed. A modest wired connection outperforms fast wifi that fluctuates. The venue side of the link is the bigger question. Our guide to [live streaming an AGM or town hall](/blog/agm-town-hall-live-streaming/) covers venue connectivity in detail.

**Should the presenter share slides through the platform?**
Preferably not. Screen-sharing compresses the content and makes it hostage to their connection. Run the slides from playback in the room and have the presenter call the cues. Cleaner picture, one less thing to fail.

**What happens if the speaker drops mid-sentence?**
With this workflow: the operator switches to the backup device or dial-in, the chair covers with the holding script, and the audience experiences a short pause. Without it: the long silence everyone remembers.

# 13. Accessible Conference AV: Captioning, Hearing Support and Inclusive Production

> **File:** `content/blog/accessible-conference-av.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-09-28 · **Target:** `accessible conference av` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Accessible conference AV doesn't work as a bolt-on ordered the week before doors. Captioning, hearing support, interpreter positioning, stage access, timing: these are production decisions, and they only get done properly when they're made early. In any sizeable audience there are delegates with hearing loss, low vision, mobility needs or sensory sensitivities. Most of them will never mention it.

This guide covers what inclusive production actually involves and the decisions to settle in your event brief: the captioning options and their honest trade-offs, how hearing support systems work, what interpreters need from staging and lighting, and the physical and timing details that make a room genuinely usable. Accessibility sits inside your wider [conference AV](/services/conference-av-hire/) specification, not beside it.

## Start with the audience, not the equipment

The single highest-value accessibility step costs nothing: ask. Put an access-requirements question on the registration form and read the answers early. Design to them, plus a sensible baseline for the needs nobody declared.

For public bodies there's also a statutory context. Irish Sign Language has been recognised in law since the Irish Sign Language Act 2017, and public bodies carry duties around ISL access to their services. If that applies to your organisation, those obligations belong in the event brief from day one. That's the same [conference AV technical brief](/blog/conference-av-technical-brief/) that carries your room, audio and screen decisions.

## Live captioning: the options honestly compared

### Human captioning

A trained captioner (the service is often called CART, communication access real-time translation) listens to the event and writes live text. It's the most accurate option. It copes with accents, names, technical jargon and crosstalk, especially when scripts and slides are sent ahead. It's a professional service booked weeks in advance, and it costs accordingly.

### Automated captioning

Speech-recognition captioning has improved enormously and costs far less. It still stumbles on proper names, Irish-language terms, heavy crosstalk and poor audio feeds. For an internal team day it may be perfectly adequate. For a public conference, an AGM or anything on the record, human or human-corrected captioning remains the safer call.

### Where the captions appear

Captions can run on the main screens as open captions everyone sees, on a dedicated screen near the stage, on delegates' own devices, or on the stream for remote attendees. Remote attendance is itself an access route, covered in our [remote presenter workflow](/blog/hybrid-remote-speaker-workflow/). Two production details decide whether captions actually work. The display must be readable (size, contrast, position) from the seats that need it. And the captioner must be fed clean audio from the microphone mix, never a laptop mic at the back of the room.

> [TAILOR: EventSound's actual captioning provision (what we supply directly versus arrange through captioning partners), and a real event where captions ran.]

## Hearing support: loops, infrared and radio, in plain English

A PA system on its own does little for many hearing-aid users. The useful sound arrives mixed with the room's reverberation. Assistive listening fixes that by sending the microphone mix directly to the listener.

- **Induction loops** transmit straight to hearing aids switched to the T (telecoil) setting. Many venues have a fixed loop installed. Never assume it works. Loops fail quietly, so test the loop with the event's microphone mix before doors.
- **Infrared (IR) and radio (RF) systems** send the mix to portable receivers worn with headsets or neck loops, which suits rooms without a fixed loop. IR needs line of sight to its emitters. RF doesn't, but it needs frequency coordination alongside the radio microphones.

Whichever system is used, two rules hold. It must be fed from the microphone mix, so it carries exactly what the PA carries. And it must be signposted at registration and in the room. A system nobody knows about helps nobody.

> [TAILOR: which hearing-assistance systems EventSound provides or sources, and typical receiver numbers for a given audience size.]

## Interpreters: sightlines, lighting and cameras

An ISL interpreter is part of the show, and production choices decide whether they can actually be seen.

- **Position** the interpreter close to the speaker, so deaf delegates can watch both without constantly switching focus.
- **Light** them steadily and evenly. And check the lighting plan doesn't plunge them into darkness during video playback or a dimmed awards moment.
- **Reserve seating** near the front with a clear sightline to the interpreter.
- **On a stream**, give the interpreter a dedicated camera and a consistent picture-in-picture, not an occasional cutaway.

Interpreters working long sessions operate in pairs and hand over every fifteen to twenty minutes, so the stage layout and run of show should allow for two.

## Stage access and the physical room

- **Step-free access to the stage.** Steps alone exclude people. A ramp needs run length, which means floor space, so it belongs in the staging plan early, not improvised on the morning.
- **Lectern flexibility.** A fixed-height lectern fails wheelchair users and shorter presenters. Plan a second, lower microphone position or an adjustable alternative.
- **Cable discipline.** Cable ramps and taped runs keep accessible routes genuinely accessible for wheels and canes.
- **Sightlines for lip readers.** Reserved seats near the front, speakers' faces lit rather than silhouetted, and no lectern placed against a blinding backlight.

> [TAILOR: EventSound's stage-access options (ramp availability and the gradients achievable with our modular staging heights), plus a real event where we built step-free access.]

## Quiet spaces and sensory planning

Conferences are loud by design. A quiet room away from the PA gives people somewhere to reset without leaving the event. Feed it the programme audio at low level, or a caption screen, and nobody has to choose between coping and content.

Announce haze, strobe or loud playback in advance and in the programme. Keep walk-in music at conversation level. Avoid full-level stings straight after quiet segments.

## A run of show that includes everyone

- Captions lag the speaker by a second or two, so chairs should hold a beat before taking responses in Q&A.
- Schedule interpreter handovers and captioner breaks. Accuracy drops when people are run into the ground.
- Make breaks long enough for people who move slowly to get refreshments and return without stress.
- Send scripts, slides and speaker names to captioners and interpreters in advance. Preparation is most of their accuracy.
- Brief the MC to describe key visuals aloud and repeat audience questions into a microphone. Each speaker should be named as they begin.

## A practical accessibility checklist

- Access-requirements question on the registration form
- Captioning method chosen (human, automated or corrected) and booked
- Caption display readable from the seats that need it: position, size, contrast
- Venue loop tested with the event mix, or portable hearing support arranged
- Hearing support fed from the microphone mix and signposted
- Interpreter position, lighting and reserved sightline seating agreed
- Dedicated interpreter camera on any stream
- Step-free stage access in the staging plan
- Lower or adjustable microphone position at the lectern
- Quiet room with a low-level programme feed
- Scripts and slides sent to captioners and interpreters
- MC and session chairs briefed

## Frequently asked questions

**How far in advance should captioners or interpreters be booked?**
Weeks, not days. Skilled captioners and ISL interpreters are in demand. Book them when you book the venue, and send materials at least a week ahead.

**Is automated captioning good enough to meet our obligations?**
It depends on the event, the audience and the audio quality you feed it. For public bodies and formal or on-the-record meetings, human or human-corrected captioning is the safer choice. If in doubt, ask the people who'll rely on it.

**Does the venue's built-in hearing loop cover us?**
Only if it works. It needs to cover the right room and be connected to the event's microphone mix too. Test it during setup rather than trusting the sticker on the door.

**What does accessibility add to the budget?**
Honestly, it ranges. Briefing, seating plans and testing cost nothing. Captioning and interpretation are professional services with real fees. What never changes: retrofitting access a week out costs more than planning it in, and delivers worse.

# 14. How to Compare AV Quotes Like-for-Like: A Procurement Guide for Event Organisers

> **File:** `content/blog/compare-av-quotes-guide.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-10-01 · **Target:** `compare av quotes` · **Tailoring spots:** 3

Say two quotes arrive for the same conference: one at €4,900, one at €7,800. Neither supplier is lying and neither number is padded. They're quoting different things. One includes a technician until midnight. The other's price quietly ends at 6pm. One carries spare microphones and backup playback. The other carries exactly what the email asked for.

There's no standard format for an AV quote, which makes "cheapest wins" a coin toss. This guide shows where the differences hide and how to compare AV quotes on a genuinely like-for-like footing. It also says, honestly, when the cheapest quote is the right choice. It's written for anyone procuring [conference AV](/services/conference-av-hire/), from a one-room seminar to a multi-day programme.

## Why AV quotes rarely compare cleanly

Suppliers price the brief they receive, and a vague brief forces every supplier to fill the gaps with their own assumptions. One assumes you need crew all day. Another assumes kit delivered and left. One reads "finish at 5pm" as the event ending, another as the get-out ending. The spread between quotes is mostly a spread between assumptions.

Two things follow from that. First, the fix starts on your side of the table. Send every supplier the same written specification (our guide to [building a conference AV technical brief](/blog/conference-av-technical-brief/) exists for exactly this reason). Second, the gaps in a cheap quote don't disappear. They come back later as extras on the invoice. Or worse, as things that simply aren't there on show day.

## Where the money hides

The underlying cost drivers (equipment, crew, rehearsals, venue constraints) are unpacked in [what actually drives conference AV costs](/blog/conference-av-costs-explained/). The procurement question is narrower. Which of them has each quote included?

### Crew hours and the operating day

The commonest gap. A quote includes crew "for the day". Whose day? Yours might run from 7am venue access to an awards finish at 11:30pm, with a get-out after that. That's a very long day, and somebody is paying for it. Either the quote that priced it, or you, later, at an overtime rate you never asked about.

Ask every supplier: how many crew, from what time to what time, what happens after that time, and is the de-rig included?

### Rehearsal time

A technical rehearsal is where failures get found while they're still free to fix. Plenty of quotes price zero rehearsal. The number looks better, and the risk quietly transfers to you. For anything with multiple presenters, video playback or a stream, rehearsal time belongs in the quote as real crew hours with the kit powered on.

### Transport, access and load-in

Delivery isn't just mileage. A venue with a loading dock and a service lift is one price. A ballroom two corridors and a goods lift away is extra crew hours. A supplier who hasn't asked about access is guessing, and "subject to site survey" after signature is where guesses get expensive. So welcome the supplier who wants to see the venue first. They're pricing reality.

### Backup equipment

The invisible line. Spare radio microphones, backup playback, redundant processing. None of it appears in the room photos, and none of it is missed until the moment it's needed. A quote without backups is cheaper right up to show day. And you can't see redundancy missing in a quote unless you ask for it by name.

> [TAILOR: a real show-day save from backup kit — what failed, what took over, and whether the audience noticed.]

### Dry hire, delivered, or operated

The same equipment can be three different products. Dry hire, where you collect and operate it. Delivered and installed. Or operated, with crew running the show. They look alike in a subject line and price entirely differently. As of August 2026, our LED wall dry hire rate is €125 per m² per day, while operated packages are quoted all-inclusive. Same wall, two different products. When quotes disagree sharply, check they're selling the same product before you check anything else.

### The small print that moves money

Consumables and batteries, damage waivers, insurance requirements, cancellation terms, weather contingency for outdoor events, and who pays if the venue's power turns out to be inadequate. None of these is large on its own. Together they move real money, and they only appear when you read past the total.

> [TAILOR: an anonymised real comparison — two quotes seen for the same event, the size of the gap, and what actually explained it.]

## How to force a like-for-like comparison

1. **Send the same written brief to every supplier.** Room, audience size, format, run of show, stream or not, rehearsal or not.
2. **Define the operating day in writing.** Access time, doors, finish time, get-out.
3. **Ask for the total cost of the event, not a kit list.** Delivery, installation, operation and de-rig, all in.
4. **Ask the sharpest question in AV procurement: "What is not included in this price?"** The quality of the answer tells you about the supplier, not just the quote.
5. **Ask each supplier to state their assumptions.** The good ones already have.
6. **Compare crew hours, not crew count.** Two technicians for twelve hours isn't two technicians for eight.
7. **Ask what backup kit will be on site**, and what the plan is if the main system fails.
8. **Get the site visit before you sign, not after.** Prices firm up when suppliers stop guessing.

## The like-for-like checklist

| Line to check | Why it matters | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment specification | "A screen" spans a 43" TV to a 20m² wall | Exact sizes, quantities and specifications |
| Crew count × hours | The largest hidden variable | Who is on site, from when to when? |
| Operating day | Overruns are billable | Is the get-out after the event included? |
| Overtime rate | Late finishes happen | What is the rate, and when does it start? |
| Rehearsal | Where failures get found cheaply | How many rehearsal hours are priced in? |
| Load-in and access | Stairs and corridors cost crew time | Has the supplier seen the venue? |
| Transport | Sometimes a line item, sometimes not | Are delivery and collection included? |
| Backup equipment | Invisible until needed | What spares are on site? |
| Operation | Dry hire and operated look alike on paper | Who runs the show, and for how long? |
| Consumables and waivers | Small lines that add up | Batteries, damage waiver, insurance terms? |
| Cancellation terms | Events move | What are the fees at 30, 14 and 7 days? |
| Exclusions | The honest quote lists them | "What is not included?" |

Take the table to each quote in turn. Where a line is silent, you haven't found a saving. You've found a question nobody has answered yet.

## When the cheapest quote is genuinely fine

Candour cuts both ways. A forty-person internal seminar with one lectern microphone, one screen and no stream carries very little production risk. A competent dry hire or a delivered-and-installed package is genuinely enough, and paying for rehearsals and layered redundancy there is over-specification, not prudence.

The way to decide is to price the failure, not the fear. If the worst case is a ten-minute delay and mild embarrassment in front of colleagues, buy simple and keep the budget. If the worst case is four hundred delegates watching a blank screen, a keynote speaker flown in for the day, or an AGM stream failing mid-vote, then the rehearsal and the backup kit are the cheapest insurance available. Cheap is only a false economy when the event was never simple to begin with.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many quotes should I get?**
Two or three against the same written brief beat five against a vague email. Comparable beats numerous.

**Should I tell suppliers my budget?**
A range, yes. It lets suppliers specify to reality instead of guessing, and guesses are what make quotes incomparable. Hiding the budget rarely produces a lower price. It produces a wider spread.

**What is the biggest red flag in an AV quote?**
No crew hours stated. Equipment without named hours means the operating day hasn't been thought about, and that conversation happens later, at a price. A close second: a supplier who quoted a complex event without asking a single question.

**Is the most expensive quote the safest?**
No. Price signals scope, not competence. Judge the stated assumptions, the answers to your questions and the willingness to survey the venue. Not the number alone.

# 15. Conference and Awards Night Lighting Design: How Professionals Light the Room

> **File:** `content/blog/event-lighting-design-guide.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-10-05 · **Target:** `conference lighting design` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Conference lighting design and awards night lighting design start from the same question. Where should the audience look, and how should the room feel at each point in the running order? Fixture choice, colour, angles and cues all follow from that. It's the difference between a room that happens to be lit and a room that's been designed.

The two formats pull apart quickly after that. A conference wants clarity that holds all day and on camera. An awards night wants states that change with the running order. This guide covers the layers that make up a rig, where the priorities separate between those two jobs, the mistakes we see most often, and exactly what to hand over when you brief a designer. If you're still writing the brief for the event as a whole, our [conference AV planning guide](/blog/conference-av-technical-brief/) has the template that sits around it.

## What a lighting designer actually decides

Four decisions get made before anyone lifts a flightcase:

- **Focus.** What gets lit: the lectern, the band, the set, the tables, the walls. And just as importantly, what stays dark.
- **Levels.** Bright enough for faces to read clearly from the back row and on camera, without flattening the room.
- **Colour.** Warm or cool white for people. Saturated colour for the space. Brand colours where they help, rather than everywhere.
- **Time.** How the looks change across the running order, and who triggers each change.

That last decision separates lighting design from a pile of hired kit. Fixtures on stands make a room brighter. A designed show moves. Walk-in, welcome, keynote, award, encore, each with its own state. That's the thinking behind our [lighting design service](/services/lighting-design/), and it applies whether the rig is four uplighters or forty fixtures.

## The layers of a conference or awards rig

Most rigs, whatever the budget, are built from the same four layers.

### Front light: people first

If the audience is there to watch someone speak, sing or accept an award, front light comes first. Even, warm light on faces from a slightly raised angle keeps presenters legible from the back of the room and on camera. Skimp here and no amount of colour rescues the result: the room looks great in photos and the keynote speaker is a silhouette.

### Colour and atmosphere: uplighting and washes

LED uplighting goes against walls, columns and set pieces. It changes the character of a room without touching the ceiling. Wash lighting does the same job for the stage and dance floor from above. Both can hold a single colour all night, or shift by segment. Warm amber for dinner, cooler tones for the awards, saturated colour for the after-party.

### Movement and specials: moving heads, gobos and follow spots

Intelligent moving heads are the multi-tool of event lighting. One fixture can be a spotlight for the keynote, a textured wash for dinner and a beam effect for the band, re-aimed from the desk rather than a ladder. Around them sit the specials. Gobo projection puts a logo or pattern on a wall or dance floor. Pin spots lift table centrepieces out of the gloom, and a follow spot handles anyone who needs tracking live across a stage.

### Control: how the show is actually run

Every layer ends up in a control desk (we programme and run shows on Chamsys), with the looks built during focus time before doors. On the night, an operator steps through cues against the running order, or busks a live set, adjusting looks by hand as the show develops. A rig without a competent operator is a piano without a pianist.

## Conference lighting design: clarity before drama

Conference lighting earns its keep quietly. First priority is presenters lit evenly wherever they stand or sit. After that, a readable screen and an audience that's comfortable for a full day.

In practice that means front light covering the lectern, the panel seating and the walk-on path, with colour temperature kept consistent so photography and camera feeds match. House levels should let delegates take notes and let the Q&A cameras find faces in the audience. Uplighting in the brand colour adds polish around the room without competing with the content on screen.

If sessions are being filmed or streamed, set levels for the camera first. A look that feels pleasantly moody in the room reads as underexposed on the recording.

An awards night is a show with dinner attached, and the lighting plan should treat it that way. The room needs distinct states: an arrival look, a dinner state warm enough to eat under, and an awards state that snaps attention to the stage.

The details carry it. A lit walk-up path, so winners aren't picking their way through darkness. A follow spot or a re-aimed moving head tracking them to the stage. ## Live shows, for contrast: rhythm and restraint

Worth a short detour, because it shows what the other two formats are not. Music-led events flip the priorities. The lighting follows the set list rather than a script, so the designer builds a palette of looks to suit each song's feel, and the operator plays them live. Moving heads do the heavy lifting. But front light still matters, because an audience wants to see faces, not just beams.

Restraint is the professional tell. Everything-flashing-at-once reads as amateur within three songs. Contrast is what makes the big moments land. Darker verses, big choruses. And if the design leans on beam looks, check the venue's policy on haze early: without atmosphere in the air, beams are largely invisible.

## Lighting around screens: don't fight your own content

Most corporate stages now carry an LED wall or a projection screen, and the lighting has to be designed around it. LED walls are bright and forgive some spill, but stray beams and badly aimed washes still cheapen the picture. Projection is far less forgiving and needs front light kept off the screen entirely.

The designer's job is balance: faces lit to a level that holds its own against the wall behind them, especially on camera. If a screen is part of your stage design, flag it in the lighting brief so the two are specified together. ## Common mistakes at conferences and awards nights

- **All colour, no key light.** Forty uplighters and nothing on faces. The room photographs beautifully and every speaker looks unlit.
- **One static look all night.** If the room looks identical at arrival and at the awards, the budget bought brightness, not design.
- **Ignoring the camera.** Levels set for the eye are usually too low for the lens. If it's being filmed, light it for the film.
- **Assuming the venue rig covers it.** House lighting is built for general use, not your show. A good supplier will integrate with it rather than duplicate it, though.
- **No dark time in the schedule.** Focusing and programming need the room dark and empty. If the schedule never allows it, the first run-through happens in front of guests.
- **Brand colour by guesswork.** "Sort of teal" produces sort-of-teal. Supply the exact colour reference and approve it against the actual walls at setup.

# 17. How Does Video Wall Hire Work? Sizes, Setup and Show Day

> **File:** `content/blog/video-wall-hire-guide.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-10-12 · **Target:** `how does video wall hire work` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

A video wall is built on site, panel by panel. That's the part most people don't picture when they imagine a big screen. Modular LED cabinets click together into one bright surface with no visible joins, at whatever size the room needs, and a processor maps your content across the lot.

Hiring one is handled over on our [LED video wall hire page](/services/led-video-walls/). This article is the explainer behind it. ## How a video wall actually works

Most rental panels are half-metre-square cabinets that lock together into a frame, so a wall is assembled in 500mm steps rather than bought in fixed sizes. Each cabinet carries tens of thousands of LED pixels. A video processor stitches the cabinets into one canvas and maps your content across it.

Because every pixel emits its own light, the image holds up in a fully lit room. That's the practical advantage over projection, which fades as the house lights rise. And unlike a bank of televisions, there are no bezels: the joins between panels are invisible at viewing distance, so a five-metre wall reads as one screen, not a grid.

On the day, the wall takes whatever you feed it. Laptop slides over HDMI, playback video, or a live camera mix for bigger rooms. The processor scales each source to the canvas.

## Video wall sizes for events

Because walls are modular, "what sizes are there" really means "what size does your room need". Builds step up in half-metre increments, and ours run from 6m² conference backdrops to 50m²+ outdoor screens. The shapes below are the ones event organisers ask for most:

| Build | Area | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| 3m × 2m | 6m² | Boardrooms, breakout rooms, product demos |
| 4m × 2.5m | 10m² | The typical single-room conference backdrop |
| 5m × 3m | 15m² | Awards nights, galas and launches |
| 6m × 3.5m | 21m² | Large conferences and wide ballrooms |
| 8m × 5m and up | 40m²+ | Outdoor stages and festival screens |

Two measurements decide the right row on that table. The distance to your furthest viewer sets how big the image must be to stay legible from the back. The ceiling height and stage width cap what physically fits. A three-metre-high wall under a 3.2m ceiling leaves no room for structure.

Ratios are flexible. Half-metre panel steps mean a build approximates 16:9 rather than hitting it exactly, and the processor maps standard content onto the canvas cleanly. # 18. What Size Stage Do I Need for an Event? Footprint, Height and Fit

> **File:** `content/blog/stage-hire-ireland-guide.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-10-15 · **Target:** `what size stage do i need for an event` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Two questions decide the size of a stage. What happens on the platform sets the footprint. Who's watching, and from where, sets the height. Get both right and the stage disappears into the event, which is exactly what a good stage should do. Get them wrong and every sightline, photograph and awards walk-up pays for it.

This guide works through the sizing in order. What a hired stage is actually made of, how to size the footprint for speakers, panels, bands and full casts, why height is the dimension organisers most often under-call, what safety kit comes with the size you land on, and the venue checks that decide whether it fits at all. It applies whether you're staging a conference in a hotel ballroom or a summer show in a field.

## What a hired stage is made of

A hired stage is a modular platform: deck sections that lock together into the footprint you need, standing on legs that set the height. Around the platform sit the parts nobody thinks to ask about. Steps on and off. Safety rails for open edges. Skirting to close in the underside so the structure reads as a stage rather than scaffolding.

Who builds it matters as much as what arrives. When you compare quotes, confirm which of those jobs each price actually covers.

## Indoor or outdoor: it changes the sums

The same TUV-certified modular system builds both, but the two jobs are planned differently.

Indoors, the environment is on your side: a level floor, shelter, house power. The constraints are physical. Door widths, lifts and corridors on the load-in route, and a ceiling tall enough for the platform and a standing presenter, with room for whatever set or screen goes behind them. Measure the route and the room before you settle on a stage size.

Outdoors, ground and weather take over the plan. Wind is what stops shows and rain is what soaks kit. The ground decides whether the build is quick or slow. Size shifts too. A standing crowd spreads back further than seated rows, so an outdoor platform usually ends up higher than the indoor equivalent for the same audience. ### Footprint: size it for the action

|---|---|---|

| Panel discussion (four or five people) | 6m × 3m | Chairs and a coffee table swallow depth fast |

| Live band or DJ | 6m × 4m upwards | A drum kit alone claims roughly 2m × 2m |
| School show, choir or dance group | 8m × 4m upwards | Large casts often work better with tiered rows |

The running order settles the final footprint: a conference stage that also hosts a five-piece band at the evening dinner is sized for the band, not the lectern. School shows catch people out the same way. The full-cast finale needs more deck than any scene before it, so size for the finale. ### Height: size it for the audience

Height is a sightline calculation, and it's the dimension organisers most often under-specify. On a flat floor with seated rows, platforms of roughly 600mm to 1m keep a presenter visible past the first few rows. Standing audiences outdoors need higher again. Cameras change the sums too. If the event is filmed or streamed, a raised platform gives the lenses a clean eyeline over seated heads, which shows up in every frame of the recording.

### Don't forget what stands on it

Lecterns, screens, banner frames and set pieces all eat footprint and height. If a screen stands on the platform, its top edge has to clear the presenter and still fit under the ceiling. ## Stage safety: what a professional build includes

Staging is one of the few parts of an event that can genuinely hurt someone, so this is the place to be fussy about your supplier.

- **A certified structure.** Our modular staging is TUV-certified, indoors and out. Whoever you hire, ask for certification evidence. Venues and insurers increasingly want to see it.
- **Safety rails.** Open edges at the back and sides should carry rails wherever the height or the activity warrants them. A seated panel three steps up is one conversation. A children's dance troupe on a metre-high platform is another.
- **Proper steps.** Steps with a handrail, positioned where people actually get on and off. Awards guests in evening wear should never be hauling themselves onto decks.
- **Skirting.** Mostly cosmetic, but it closes in the underside, keeps stored cases and cable runs out of sight, and stops the front row studying the void beneath the drummer.

## Access, build time and what to check with your venue

# 19. The Outdoor Stage Planning Checklist: Ground, Rain and Wind in Ireland

> **File:** `content/blog/outdoor-stage-hire-ireland.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-10-19 · **Target:** `outdoor stage planning checklist ireland` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

An outdoor stage is less about the platform than about everything around it. The ground it stands on, the weather it'll take, and the wind plan for the day the forecast turns. An indoor stage borrows its venue's floor, power and shelter. An outdoor stage brings all of that with it, or does without.

So plan one as a checklist rather than a purchase. This guide is that checklist, in the order the questions come up on an Irish site. Ground and access first, then rain cover, then the wind call, then power and certification, then a build timeline that survives contact with the weather. The last section pulls it into one list you can paste into an email before anyone prices the job. For footprint and height, start with our guide to [what size stage you need](/blog/stage-hire-ireland-guide/).

## Why outside is a different job

An indoor venue lends your stage its floor, its power, its shelter and its walls. A field lends you nothing. Everything the show needs has to arrive on a truck, stand up to the forecast and come back down on schedule. Which is why the platform itself is the easy part.

The kit is the same place to start, though: our [staging and pipe and drape service](/services/staging-pipe-drape/) uses the same TUV-certified modular decks indoors and out, with adjustable legs, safety rails, steps and skirting. What changes outside is the planning wrapped around the build. And that planning is most of the job, which is why the rest of this reads as a list of decisions rather than a spec sheet.

## Start with the ground

The first questions we ask about any outdoor stage are about the ground, because the ground decides the build before the weather says a word.

- **Slope.** Almost no outdoor site is truly level. Adjustable legs take out the slope a lawn or a yard actually has. Each leg is set to the ground beneath it, so the platform comes up level.
- **Softness.** Grass after a wet week behaves nothing like tarmac. Soft ground slows a build and matters for anything heavy standing on or beside the deck, so tell your supplier what the site turns into after rain.
- **Access.** How close can a van or truck get to the stage position? Every metre between tailgate and platform is carried by hand, and long carries stretch build days and budgets.

Send photos with your enquiry. Phone shots of the site from a few angles do more than a map pin. Site visits are free for confirmed bookings, and for a tricky site one is worth doing before the schedule gets locked.

## Rain: plan for it rather than hope

In Ireland rain is a when, not an if, so an outdoor stage plan starts by assuming it. Three questions matter.

**Does the stage need cover?** A short speech at a fun day might risk the sky. A band with instruments, monitors and mains power on deck should not. Decide early, because cover changes the structure, the budget and the wind plan below.

**What about the kit that isn't on stage?** The mix position, power distribution and cable runs need protection too, and they're the items rain finds first.

**Screens are the exception.** Our outdoor LED panels are weather-rated, which is one half of why LED walls rather than projection are the outdoor standard. Daylight is the other half.

> [TAILOR: the cover options we provide or bring in for outdoor stages, with a photo of a covered build]

## Wind: the one that actually stops shows

Rain makes an outdoor show uncomfortable. Wind is what makes the call. A stage carrying a roof, backdrops or banners has real wind load to manage (drape and scrim behave like sails), so wind planning is structural, not cosmetic.

In practice that means three things. Strip the sail area you don't need: the banners that look harmless in a sketch are the first thing a gust argues with. Watch the forecast from the week out, not the morning of. And agree in advance what happens if the wind gets up: what comes down, what gets delayed and, above all, who makes the call. A named decision-maker beats a huddle at the worst possible moment.

## Power and a safe site

Outdoor power is either the venue's supply or a generator, and the stage position is often a long way from both. Flag the distance in your enquiry: cable runs need routing where the public and vehicles won't find them, and connections need to live up off the grass.

The safety standard doesn't relax because the venue is a field. The staging is TUV-certified, open edges carry rails where the height warrants them, steps go where performers actually enter, and the crew who install the stage check it before anyone stands on it. Public events also mean paperwork. Our clients include Fingal County Council and Louth County Council, and any supplier should be able to produce certification evidence for the structure the moment it's asked for.

## The build: how an outdoor stage comes together

A straightforward outdoor job runs: site questions or a visit, then the quote, then build day, show day and derig. Outdoor schedules need more margin than indoor ones (weather windows, ground conditions and site traffic all move), so be wary of any plan where the stage finishes minutes before soundcheck.

Our quotes are all-inclusive: equipment, delivery, installation, on-site operation and breakdown. That means the build crew and the derig are in the figure from the start rather than surprises at the end. And the sequence scales. **Can you build a stage on grass or on a slope?**
Yes. Adjustable legs level the platform on sloping or uneven ground, and grass is routine. What helps is honesty about the site early on: photos, the slope, and what the ground does after rain.

**What happens if the weather turns on the day?**

# 20. LED Screen vs LED Wall: Which One Your Event Needs

> **File:** `content/blog/led-screen-or-led-wall.md` · **Publishes:** 2026-10-22 · **Target:** `led screen vs led wall` · **Tailoring spots:** 4

Somewhere between a 43" screen on a stand and a six-metre LED wall sits the right display for your event. LED screen vs LED wall is a simpler decision than it usually sounds. If your audience sits within seven or eight metres of the picture (a boardroom, a breakout room, an exhibition stand), a [single large-format screen](/services/led-screen-hire/) between 43" and 98" covers it. If you're filling a main stage, putting graphics behind presenters for hundreds of people, or working outdoors, you're in [LED video wall](/services/led-video-walls/) territory. This guide sets out when each is the right call, and how to judge the grey area in between.

## The short answer

Use this as the router. The rest of the guide is the reasoning.

- **Boardroom, training day or hybrid meeting:** one screen, sized to the room.
- **Conference breakout rooms:** single screens, quick to rig and easy to repeat across rooms.
- **Exhibition stand:** one large, bright screen (75" to 98") at eye level.
- **Town hall or all-hands up to roughly 100 people:** one or two big screens.
- **Main conference stage, awards night or launch for 150+:** a modular LED wall.
- **Anything outdoors:** weather-rated wall panels, whatever the size.

If a supplier's recommendation cuts against this pattern, ask why. There are good reasons to cross the line. But there should be a reason.

## What separates a screen from a wall

An LED screen, in hire terms, is a single large-format display. A commercial-grade television, in other words. Ours run from 43" to 98" and go up on floor stands, table mounts or wall brackets. One unit, one power lead, one signal cable: it's working minutes after it arrives.

An LED wall is modular. Individual panels (we stock Absen P2.6 and Unilumin) bolt together into whatever size and shape the design needs, from 6m² behind a conference stage to 50m²+ on an outdoor stage. A wall can be ground-supported or rigged, flat, curved or angled. It's a small construction project. It needs build time, and it needs a technician.

Plug-and-play unit versus engineered build. That difference drives the cost, the setup time and the crew each involves.

## When a single LED screen is the right call

Screens win wherever the audience is close and the room is modest. Boardrooms and hybrid meetings are the clearest case: a screen with a Teams, Zoom or Google Meet setup turns a meeting room into a working hybrid space with no production footprint. Breakout rooms at conferences want the same thing. Slides or a feed from the main room, on a screen that was rigged before breakfast.

Exhibition stands are the third natural home. A bright 98" screen at eye level does more for a three-metre stand than most custom builds, and it packs away in minutes when the show closes.

Sizing is viewing-distance arithmetic. For slides and presentation content, the furthest viewer should sit within roughly six times the screen height. A 98" screen is about 1.2 metres tall, so it holds to around seven metres back. That's real depth for a flat-floor room. A 43" runs out of legibility past about three metres, which is why it belongs on a boardroom table rather than a stage.

Nor are screens only the small-event option. We've supplied single LED screens, not walls, for the TG4 Conference in Galway and the Music Awards at the Hillgrove Hotel in Monaghan. Past a certain room size a screen stops working. But before that point it's the correct answer, not the compromise.

## When only a modular wall will do

Walls earn their build time in three situations.

**Scale.** Once the audience passes 150 or the room gets deep, no single screen reaches the back rows. A wall is built to the size the sightlines demand rather than the size a factory makes. 4m × 2.5m, 6m × 3m, or wider still. The spec follows the room, too: pixel pitch (ours run 1.9mm–3.9mm) sets how close the image stays sharp, and our plain-English guide to [pixel pitch and viewing distance](/blog/pixel-pitch-explained/) shows how to match it.

**The stage picture.** At conferences, awards nights and launches, the screen increasingly is the set: full-width branding, speaker names, category graphics, sponsor content. A wall does this. A television on a stand cannot. We built the LED wall and conference AV for the School Summit at the Eikon Arena, and a curved LED video wall for the Beta Festival at The Digital Hub in Dublin. One caveat: full-stage graphics need designing for the wall's exact shape. Our guide to [how to prepare presentation content for a big LED wall](/blog/led-wall-presentation-content/) covers aspect ratios and safe areas.

**Outdoors.** Daylight demands brightness a domestic-style panel cannot give, and Irish weather demands sealed, weather-rated panels. The outdoor LED screen we supply for the Swords Castle Summer Concerts plays to more than 8,000 people a night. Modular panels, built for the site.

## The grey area: 90 to 150 people

The genuinely debatable zone is the flat-floor event for 90 to 150 people. The town hall, the seminar, the single-room conference. Both options work. They just buy different things.

Two 75" or 98" screens flanking the stage give everyone a clear copy of the content for less money and far less setup time. Choose screens when the content is presentations, the room is wide rather than deep, and the budget has better uses elsewhere.

A small wall (say 3m × 2m) gives one unbroken picture with genuine presence: full-bleed graphics and a backdrop that photographs properly. Choose the wall when the event is a brand moment rather than a meeting. A launch, an awards segment, anything that'll be filmed.

Two large screens come in meaningfully lighter. If the extra spend buys nothing the event needs, the screens are the better job. And we'll say so at quote stage.

## Common mistakes

- **Hiring a wall for a boardroom.** You pay for build time, crew and panels the room can't use.
- **One mid-sized screen for a deep room.** Past six times the screen height, the back half stops reading and gives up.
- **Ignoring ceiling height and rigging.** A three-metre-high wall has to stand or hang somewhere. Check the room before you approve the render.
- **Trusting the venue's house TV.** It's usually too small for the room, too dim for daylight, and missing the input you need at 8.55am.
