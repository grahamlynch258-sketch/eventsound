As a conference grows, three things change first. The sound has to reach every seat evenly, not just the front rows. The screens have to be readable from the very back. And the room needs more people running the technical side, because more things are happening at once. A conference of around 100 can often get by with a simple, well-placed setup. At around 200 the stage, the screens and the microphones start to matter much more. At around 500 you're planning a production, with filming or live vision and a crew that works as a team.

If you're weighing up your own event, our [conference AV hire service](/services/conference-av-hire/) page covers what we supply. This guide explains how the needs shift with audience size, and why headcount is only part of the picture.

## The three sizes side by side

This table is deliberately general. Every room is different, and the sections below explain the reasoning.

| What | Around 100 | Around 200 | Around 500 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sound** | Speakers placed so every seat hears clearly | Speakers covering the back rows as well as the front | A properly designed PA that covers the whole room evenly |
| **Screens** | A screen everyone can read from the back row | A larger screen, or more than one, so the back rows can read the small text | Large screens, often with the presenter shown live on them |
| **Microphones** | Lectern plus a handheld or lavalier | A microphone for each speaker, plus audience questions | Several wireless microphones, with spares ready |
| **Lighting** | Enough to light the presenter and the room | Presenter lit clearly, with the room lit to match | Stage and room lighting designed together |
| **Staging** | May not need a stage if sightlines are good | A stage is usually worth it so the back rows can see | A stage is usually needed, built to suit the room |
| **Filming or streaming** | One camera, if wanted | Camera and vision mixing if filmed or streamed | Multi-camera filming and a dedicated vision operator |
| **Crew** | One technician can often manage | A small team, with sound and vision handled separately | A larger team with clear roles |

## Around 100 people

A room of 100 is the size where simple choices still work. The room is usually a hotel function room or a meeting space, and the audience is close enough to the front that everyone can see and hear without much help.

**Sound.** The aim is clear speech. In a room this size, a pair of speakers placed well often does the job. What matters is that the people at the back hear the same level and clarity as the people at the front. A lectern microphone and a handheld or lavalier (a small clip-on microphone) cover most programmes.

**Screens.** Presentations need a screen big enough that the smallest text on a slide can be read from the last row. For a room like this, that may be an individual LED screen or TV on a stand. Our individual screens run from 43" to 98". If you're also deciding between a screen and a wall, our guide on [choosing between an LED screen and an LED wall](/blog/led-screen-or-led-wall/) sets out the trade-offs.

**Staging.** A low-ceilinged room with good sightlines may not need a stage at all. If chairs are in rows and the presenter would be hidden by the heads in front, a small stage solves it.

**Crew.** Even at this size, someone has to run the sound and the slides. Every AV hire from us includes a technician on site, so you're never left operating the kit yourself.

## Around 200 people

At 200 the room is bigger, the back rows are further from the front, and small problems become visible. A mumbled word or a slide with tiny text affects far more people.

**Sound.** The back of the room is where sound problems show up first. Speakers that sound fine near the front can leave the back rows straining. At this size you need to plan coverage so the whole room hears evenly. We supply professional line array and point source PA systems, and which suits depends on the shape of the room.

**Microphones.** This is where speaker numbers start to matter. A panel session needs a microphone for each speaker, and audience questions need a roving microphone so everyone can hear them. Our wireless rigs use Shure and Sennheiser microphones and support 30+ simultaneous channels, so there is room for every speaker and for spares.

**Screens.** With 200 seats, the back row is a long way from the screen. Text that is fine at 100 can be unreadable. One larger screen, or a pair of screens so nobody is watching from a sharp angle, becomes worth considering. We also supply LED walls from 6 m² where the room and the content call for one.

**Staging and lighting.** A stage becomes more useful, because it lets the back rows see the presenter. Lighting also matters more. A presenter who is clearly lit is easier to follow, and it keeps the room from feeling flat.

**Filming and crew.** If the event is filmed or streamed, a camera operator and a vision operator are separate jobs from the sound operator. One person can't safely do all three while a live programme runs.

## Around 500 people

At 500 you're running a production, whatever you call it. The room is large, the stage is far from the back, and a failure is visible to everyone.

**Sound.** Coverage is the main design problem. The PA has to deliver clear speech across the whole room, not just near the stage. Presenters and panellists on stage need monitors so they can hear themselves and each other. We supply line array and point source systems and monitors, and the choice depends on the room.

**Screens.** The person on stage will look small from the back row. That is what IMAG is for. It stands for image magnification, which means putting a live camera feed of the presenter on the screens so everyone sees them. A large screen or LED wall behind or beside the stage carries both slides and live video. Our walls run from 6 m² up to 100 m²+, with pixel pitch (the spacing between the LEDs, which affects how close you can sit before the image breaks up) from 1.9 mm to 3.9 mm.

**Microphones.** A bigger programme usually means more speakers, more panels and more microphones in use at once. Headset and lavalier microphones keep presenters' hands free. Handhelds work for audience questions. Spares are standard practice at this size.

**Staging and lighting.** A stage is usually needed so the whole room can see the presenters. Our staging is TUV-certified modular staging, with safety rails, skirting and steps. Lighting is designed with the stage and the room together, so the presenters stand out and the screens aren't washed out by the lights.

**Filming and streaming.** If it is filmed, multi-camera filming with live vision mixing is the norm at this size. We also stream to YouTube, Vimeo, Teams, Zoom and custom RTMP. For streaming, we recommend a minimum of 10 Mbps dedicated upload, and we bring redundant internet (wired and bonded cellular) in case the venue's connection fails.

**Crew.** More happens at once, so more people are needed. Typically that means a sound operator, a vision operator and a camera operator, as well as the people who build and break down. How many depends on the show.

## What matters more than headcount

Two conferences of 200 can need very different setups. These factors often shift the specification more than the number of seats.

**Room shape and ceiling height.** A long, narrow room puts the back rows far from the stage. A wide, shallow room spreads the audience across the front. A low ceiling limits where screens, lighting and speakers can go, and can cut off sightlines if you add a stage. A high ceiling lets screens sit higher, where the back rows can see them over the heads in front.

**Daylight.** Screens are harder to read in a bright room. If your venue has large windows, tell us. It can affect where screens go and how bright they need to be.

**Number of presenters and panel sessions.** One keynote speaker with slides needs very little. A day of panels with six speakers each needs a microphone per person, careful mixing and someone watching the levels.

**Filmed or streamed.** Once a conference is being recorded or broadcast, you need cameras, vision mixing and a stable connection, whatever the headcount.

**Remote speakers.** A speaker joining by video call means another source to manage and another sound path. We run Teams, Zoom and Google Meet hybrid setups. Test the connection before the day, not on it.

**Set-up time.** A tight morning load-in changes what is sensible. More time means more can be done carefully, and rehearsal fits in. If the room is only available shortly before doors, tell us early.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a stage for 100 people?**
Not always. If the room is small and the sightlines are good, the presenter can stand at the front without one. If the audience sits in rows and the back rows can't see, a stage helps. A site visit settles it quickly.

**Do I need more than one microphone at a conference?**
For anything beyond a single speaker, yes. A panel session needs a microphone for each speaker, and audience questions need a roving microphone so the room can hear them. Spares are sensible. A microphone with a flat battery shouldn't stop the programme.

**Should we film or stream a conference of 200?**
It depends on who needs to see it. If remote colleagues, members or sponsors would benefit, it is worth doing. It adds cameras, vision mixing and a good connection, so decide early.

**Can we start with a simple setup and add to it later?**
It's usually better to plan the whole technical side at once. Sound, screens, lighting and staging affect each other, and adding items late can mean redoing the layout. Tell us what might be added and we can allow for it.
