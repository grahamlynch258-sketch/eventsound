An outdoor stage is less about the platform than about everything around it. The ground it stands on, the weather it'll take, and the wind plan for the day the forecast turns. An indoor stage borrows its venue's floor, power and shelter. An outdoor stage brings all of that with it, or does without.

So plan one as a checklist rather than a purchase. This guide is that checklist, in the order the questions come up on an Irish site. Ground and access first, then rain cover, then the wind call, then power and certification, then a build timeline that survives contact with the weather. The last section pulls it into one list you can paste into an email before anyone prices the job. ## Why outside is a different job

An indoor venue lends your stage its floor, its power, its shelter and its walls. A field lends you nothing. Everything the show needs has to arrive on a truck, stand up to the forecast and come back down on schedule. Which is why the platform itself is the easy part.

The kit is the same place to start, though: our [staging and pipe and drape service](/services/staging-pipe-drape/) uses the same TUV-certified modular decks indoors and out, with adjustable legs, safety rails, steps and skirting. What changes outside is the planning wrapped around the build. And that planning is most of the job, which is why the rest of this reads as a list of decisions rather than a spec sheet.

## Start with the ground

The first questions we ask about any outdoor stage are about the ground, because the ground decides the build before the weather says a word.

- **Slope.** Almost no outdoor site is truly level. Adjustable legs take out the slope a lawn or a yard actually has. Each leg is set to the ground beneath it, so the platform comes up level.
- **Softness.** Grass after a wet week behaves nothing like tarmac. Soft ground slows a build and matters for anything heavy standing on or beside the deck, so tell your supplier what the site turns into after rain.
- **Access.** How close can a van or truck get to the stage position? Every metre between tailgate and platform is carried by hand, and long carries stretch build days and budgets.

Send photos with your enquiry. Phone shots of the site from a few angles do more than a map pin. Site visits are free for confirmed bookings, and for a tricky site one is worth doing before the schedule gets locked.

## Rain: plan for it rather than hope

In Ireland rain is a when, not an if, so an outdoor stage plan starts by assuming it. Three questions matter.

**Does the stage need cover?** A short speech at a fun day might risk the sky. A band with instruments, monitors and mains power on deck should not. Decide early, because cover changes the structure, the budget and the wind plan below.

**What about the kit that isn't on stage?** The mix position, power distribution and cable runs need protection too, and they're the items rain finds first.

**Screens are the exception.** Our outdoor LED panels are weather-rated, which is one half of why LED walls rather than projection are the outdoor standard. Daylight is the other half.

## Wind: the one that actually stops shows

Rain makes an outdoor show uncomfortable. Wind is what makes the call. A stage carrying a roof, backdrops or banners has real wind load to manage (drape and scrim behave like sails), so wind planning is structural, not cosmetic.

In practice that means three things. Strip the sail area you don't need: the banners that look harmless in a sketch are the first thing a gust argues with. Watch the forecast from the week out, not the morning of. And agree in advance what happens if the wind gets up: what comes down, what gets delayed and, above all, who makes the call. A named decision-maker beats a huddle at the worst possible moment.

## Power and a safe site

Outdoor power is either the venue's supply or a generator, and the stage position is often a long way from both. Flag the distance in your enquiry: cable runs need routing where the public and vehicles won't find them, and connections need to live up off the grass.

The safety standard doesn't relax because the venue is a field. The staging is TUV-certified, open edges carry rails where the height warrants them, steps go where performers actually enter, and the crew who install the stage check it before anyone stands on it. Public events also mean paperwork. Our clients include Fingal County Council and Louth County Council, and any supplier should be able to produce certification evidence for the structure the moment it's asked for.

## The build: how an outdoor stage comes together

A straightforward outdoor job runs: site questions or a visit, then the quote, then build day, show day and derig. Outdoor schedules need more margin than indoor ones (weather windows, ground conditions and site traffic all move), so be wary of any plan where the stage finishes minutes before soundcheck.

## The outdoor stage planning checklist

Put these in your first email and any supplier can quote properly:

- Exact location, and what the stage stands on: grass, tarmac, gravel, slope
- Audience size, and whether they're standing or seated
- What happens on stage: band, speeches, dance groups, awards
- Whether the stage needs cover
- Power on site: venue supply, generator, or nothing yet
- How close a vehicle can get to the stage position
- Dates and times: build access, show time, and when the site must be clear
- Who owns weather decisions on the day

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you build a stage on grass or on a slope?**
Yes. Adjustable legs level the platform on sloping or uneven ground, and grass is routine. What helps is honesty about the site early on: photos, the slope, and what the ground does after rain.

**What happens if the weather turns on the day?**
The plan is agreed before build day: what gets covered, what waits, what comes down, and who makes the call. Weather rarely cancels a well-planned outdoor event. It reshuffles it.

**When should an outdoor stage be booked?**
With the rest of the production, not after it. Stage size affects screens, sound and power, so the earlier the stage is settled, the less gets re-planned around it. And summer weekends concentrate demand on outdoor kit and crews.
