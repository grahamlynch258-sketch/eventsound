Eight thousand people a night in the grounds of a medieval castle makes for a brilliant concert — and an unforgiving production site. There is no house PA, no built-in power, no loading dock, and no shelter for equipment that cannot handle Irish weather.

EventSound delivered the outdoor LED screen and [event production](/services/event-production/) for the Swords Castle Summer Concerts, where artists including Nathan Carter played to crowds of 8,000+ per night in 2025. This case study covers the brief, the constraints of a walled heritage site, and what outdoor concert production at this scale actually involves — along with the lessons any organiser of a large outdoor event can take from it.

## The brief

The Swords Castle Summer Concerts bring major touring artists into the centre of Swords, north County Dublin. The core of the brief was simple to state and demanding to deliver: every one of 8,000+ attendees should be able to see and follow the performance, including the people standing furthest from the stage.

At this scale, a screen is not decoration. Beyond the front section of a standing crowd, the performer becomes a small figure in the distance, and the audience experience depends on the video presentation as much as the sound.

## The venue and technical constraints

Swords Castle is a walled medieval site in the heart of Swords. As a concert setting it is atmospheric and genuinely distinctive. As a production site, it behaves nothing like an arena.

A heritage venue like this shapes the technical plan before a single case is loaded in:

- **Nothing attaches to the fabric of the site.** Screens and technical structures must be freestanding and ballasted rather than rigged from walls or permanent points.
- **There is no built-in event infrastructure.** Power, cabling routes and backstage facilities all arrive temporarily and must leave without a trace.
- **Access was never designed for trucks.** Load-in routes, vehicle sizes, timings and ground protection have to be planned rather than assumed.
- **Weather is a planning assumption, not a surprise.** Outdoor LED, support structures and power distribution are specified for wind and rain from day one.

## The production approach

Large outdoor shows are won or lost in the planning. Three principles drove the approach at Swords Castle.

**Specify from the site, not from a catalogue.** A screen that looks superb indoors can be unwatchable in direct summer daylight. An outdoor concert screen needs weather-rated panels, high brightness and a support structure engineered for wind — so the site conditions drive the specification, not the other way around.

**Plan the build backwards from doors.** With thousands of people arriving at a fixed time, every technical system must be tested and signed off before gates open. The schedule works backwards from that point: screen built and tested, power proven under load, communications checked.

**Carry contingency on site.** With 8,000 people a night, there is no acceptable version of "we'll sort it tomorrow". Backup equipment for critical systems travels with the show, and faults get swapped out rather than repaired mid-event.

## How the show was delivered

A multi-night concert series settles into a rhythm: checks, soundcheck, doors, show, secure the site, repeat. The discipline is treating every night like the first one — the same tests, in the same order, signed off before gates.

During the show, the job is calm execution. Vision on the screen keeps the back of the site connected to the performance while the crew watch the systems rather than the stage. Good production work at events like this is invisible: the audience remembers the artist, not the equipment.

## What event organisers can learn from it

**Unusual venues can host major events — if you budget for what they lack.** A castle, park or civic space has no event infrastructure. Power, screens, PA and access all arrive as temporary systems, and they belong in the budget from the first draft.

**The screen is the audience experience past the front section.** Specify screen size and brightness from the site layout and viewing distances, not from what looks impressive in a brochure.

**Weather-rating is a specification decision, not a show-day one.** By the time the forecast turns, the only protection that counts is the kind engineered in weeks earlier.

**Fixed deadlines should drive the schedule.** Doors do not move. Build the technical schedule backwards from them, with testing time protected rather than treated as slack.

**Contingency is part of the specification.** Backup equipment for critical systems is what turns a potential show-stopper into a footnote.
