If you have one presenter, a laptop and a slide deck, the venue's own AV is usually enough. Take it and save yourself the extra planning. Bring an outside supplier when the event needs someone to run it: several microphones, an LED wall, filming or streaming, remote speakers, or awards-style cues where music, video and lighting change on the beat.

The dividing line is rarely the size of the room. It is whether anything has to be operated while the event is running. A house system is built to be switched on. A show has to be run.

This guide covers what in-house AV normally includes, when it is enough, and what to ask the venue before you commit. It also explains how an outside supplier fits in alongside the house system. If you are already leaning towards outside help, our [conference AV hire](/services/conference-av-hire/) page shows what we supply.

## What "in-house AV" usually means

When a hotel or conference venue says it has AV, it usually means a fixed setup that comes with the room. Typically that is a screen or a projector, a laptop connection, ceiling or wall speakers and a lectern microphone. Some venues add a couple of handheld or clip-on radio microphones. Others have a basic lighting preset for the room.

Some venues also have a preferred supplier. This is an outside company the venue works with regularly, brought in when the house kit is not enough. Ask whether "in-house" in your quote means the venue's own equipment, its own staff, or a preferred supplier. Those are three different things, and they behave differently on the day.

It also helps to know who operates the system. Some venues have a technician on site who sets up and stays for the session. Others hand you the remote and a phone number. Neither is wrong, but they suit different events.

## When in-house AV is enough

For plenty of events the house system is the right choice, and paying for more would be wasted money. It tends to work well when:

- one presenter speaks at a time, from a lectern, with slides
- the audience is small enough that the room's speakers cover everyone
- nothing is being recorded or streamed
- there are no video clips with sound, or only one or two that you can test beforehand
- nobody needs to be brought in remotely
- the session is a straightforward meeting, training day, briefing or short AGM

The advantage is real. The kit is already installed and the venue knows how it behaves in that room. There is one fewer company to coordinate, and one fewer set of load-in times to arrange.

Even then, test it. Ask to bring your slides on the laptop you will use, check the connection and play any video with sound. Do this before the day, or at least before doors open. Most house-system problems are small, and they are all much easier to sort out in a quiet room.

## When to bring your own supplier

An outside supplier earns its place when the event needs more than a system that has been switched on. These are the situations where we would suggest looking beyond the house kit.

### A larger audience

Ceiling speakers and a single screen are designed for a particular room size. As the audience grows, people at the back struggle to see slides and hear speakers. A bigger room often needs properly placed speakers and a screen sized for the back row. The fixed system in a function room is not usually built for that.

### Panels and several microphones

A panel discussion needs a microphone for each speaker, plus handheld microphones for questions from the floor. Someone also has to manage the mix so voices don't clash or squeal. One lectern microphone does not cover this. Our wireless microphone rigs can handle 30+ simultaneous channels, so a panel and audience questions are well inside what we can supply. Our guide to the [conference AV technical brief](/blog/conference-av-technical-brief/) shows how to write down what the room needs.

### An LED wall

If you want an LED wall behind the stage, that is separate equipment that has to be brought in, built, powered and run. A house screen or projector will not do that job, and it is worth asking the venue what it can and cannot accommodate.

### Filming or live streaming

Recording a conference or streaming an AGM means cameras, vision mixing and a reliable connection. Streaming also needs enough upload bandwidth, and we recommend a minimum of 10Mbps dedicated upload. A venue's guest Wi-Fi is shared with everyone else in the building and is not a safe bet. We bring redundant internet, wired plus bonded cellular, for events we stream.

### Remote speakers

Having a speaker join through Teams, Zoom or Google Meet changes the setup. The remote person has to be heard and seen in the room, and the room has to be heard and seen by them. Without someone managing that, the result is echo and awkward silences.

### Awards-style cues

Awards nights run on timing. Walk-on music, winner photos on screen, video packages and lighting changes all need to land together. That calls for someone at a desk running the show, with a script, for the whole event. A house system on its own puts no one in that seat.

### Anything where someone must operate the show

This is the simplest test. If you can picture a person needed at a desk during the event, mixing audio, switching a camera or calling a cue, then you need an operator. Our standard hire is operated, with a technician on site for the event. If you take the house system, ask who fills that role.

## What to ask the venue before you decide

Whatever you choose, a few questions are worth putting to the venue in writing. The answers settle most of the decision.

- **May an outside supplier come in?** Venues differ. Some are relaxed about it, some have a preferred supplier, and some set conditions.
- **Is there a charge or conditions for bringing one in?** Some venues charge a fee or set requirements. Ask early, and don't assume either way.
- **When can we get in?** Ask about access and load-in times, and when the room must be clear afterwards. A morning conference with a ballroom used the evening before leaves a narrow window.
- **How do we get equipment into the room?** Ask about loading access, lifts and stairs.
- **What power is available, and where?** Check the sockets and their location.
- **Are there rigging points?** If anything needs to hang, ask what the ceiling allows and who signs it off.
- **Who controls the house lighting?** Ask whether it is a simple preset or something a technician has to operate, and whether a supplier's lighting can work alongside it.
- **What internet can we use for streaming?** Ask for a wired connection, not shared Wi-Fi.
- **Who operates the house system on the day?** If someone does, ask what they will and won't do.

A venue that answers these clearly is usually a venue that has done this before. Keep the replies, because they matter when you compare quotes. Our guide to [comparing AV quotes like for like](/blog/compare-av-quotes-guide/) covers how to do that.

## How an outside supplier works alongside the house system

Hiring an outside company does not mean throwing out the house kit. We integrate with existing house rigs in venues nationwide, and often the sensible plan uses a little of both.

In general, there are two ways it can go. The outside supplier can run its own system alongside the house one, with the house kit simply left alone. Or the two can be connected, for example by sending sound to the room's own speakers, so that everything works together. Which is possible depends on the venue and the room, and the venue's technical contact is the person to speak to.

Either way, it helps if one person on the event side is the point of contact, and if the supplier and the venue speak directly well before the day. Agree who sets up first, who has the room at what time, and who to call if something doesn't work. Many problems at venues come from nobody having agreed who does what.

If you can, arrange a site visit. Our site visits are free for confirmed bookings, and seeing the room with the stage, power and access in front of you answers questions no email can.

## A short decision checklist

Take the house AV if most of these are true:

- one speaker at a time, with slides
- a small audience that the room's speakers cover
- no recording, no streaming and no remote speakers
- no LED wall and no complicated lighting
- the venue confirms who sets up and who is on hand

Bring your own supplier if any of these are true:

- a panel, or audience questions that need microphones
- an LED wall, or filming and live streaming
- a remote speaker joining online
- awards-style cues or a timed running order
- you need someone operating the show, not just a system switched on

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the house system and an outside supplier together?**
Often, yes. A common arrangement is to keep the house screen or speakers for part of the day and bring in an outside supplier for the main session. Ask the venue what it will allow, and agree who is responsible for what.

**Will the venue say no to an outside supplier?**
Some venues have a preferred supplier and some set conditions, and a few may not allow an outsider at all. Ask early, in writing, before you commit to either route.

**Is the house system always cheaper?**
Not necessarily. What each option includes varies, so compare what is actually covered rather than the headline total. That means crew, operation, setup and anything extra.

**Should I check the house AV before the event?**
Yes. Ask for a test with your own laptop and slides, and play any video that has sound. Do it in the room, ideally before the day.
