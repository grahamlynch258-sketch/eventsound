Everyone has watched it happen. The remote keynote appears on the big screen, asks "can you hear me?", hears their own voice come back a second late, and stumbles. Remote speakers at a conference fail for predictable reasons, and every one of them is preventable — not with better luck, but with a production workflow instead of a laptop balanced on the lectern.

This guide sets out that workflow: a connection test, a rehearsal call, return video so the presenter can see the room, mix-minus audio so they never hear an echo, a tested backup and a named operator. It applies to a single remote panellist just as much as a headline keynote. If you are planning a fully hybrid or streamed event, our [virtual events](/services/virtual-events/) page covers the wider production setup.

## Why remote speakers go wrong

The room and the platform are two different sound and picture worlds. The room has a PA system, microphones, screens and an audience. The platform has one webcam, one microphone and whatever the presenter's broadband is doing that afternoon. Connecting the two properly is an integration job, and when nobody owns that job, the join is exactly where things break.

The classic failures are worth naming, because the whole workflow exists to remove them:

- **Echo.** The speaker hears their own voice coming back from the room, delayed, and cannot form a sentence.
- **Talking over people.** They cannot hear the room clearly, miss the chair's cues and interrupt.
- **The frozen face.** A connection drops with no fallback, and the audience watches a pixelated still frame while someone fiddles with a laptop.
- **Presenting into the void.** With no view of the audience, the speaker's energy dies inside two minutes.

## The workflow, step by step

### 1. A connection test the week before

Every remote presenter gets a short test call on the platform the event will actually use, not whichever one they prefer. The test covers upload stability, camera framing, lighting, background noise and — most importantly — the microphone. An inexpensive wired headset beats a laptop microphone in an echoey home office every time.

The test call is also where practical details get locked in: the room they will present from, wired ethernet rather than wifi where possible, and two phone numbers in case everything else fails.

### 2. A rehearsal call on the real system

The tech check proves the connection works; the rehearsal proves the session works. It covers the handovers: who introduces the speaker, who advances the slides and on what cue, how audience questions will reach them, what the timing signals are, and what happens if they drop. The best rehearsal happens on show morning through the actual room system, so the presenter experiences the real return feed and real audio rather than a simulation.

### 3. Return video: let the speaker see the room

In-room presenters get a confidence monitor. Remote presenters need the equivalent: a return video feed showing the stage or audience and, ideally, the programme output — what the audience is actually seeing at that moment.

With return video, the speaker sees the MC welcome them, knows when their slides are live, reads the room's reaction and can pause for a laugh instead of ploughing through it. Without it, they are performing into a black hole, and it shows.

### 4. Mix-minus audio, in plain English

The awkward echo has one specific cause. If you send the presenter the full room mix, that mix includes their own voice, delayed by the round trip. Hearing yourself half a second late is genuinely disorientating; nobody can speak fluently through it.

The fix is called mix-minus: the presenter receives everything from the room — the chair, the other panellists, audience questions — minus their own voice. Every competent hybrid setup runs one. If you only ask a prospective AV supplier one technical question, ask how they handle mix-minus for remote contributors. The answer tells you a lot.

### 5. A backup that has already been tested

Broadband rarely fails, but "rarely" is not a plan. A workable backup chain looks like this: a second device on a different network — typically a phone on 4G or 5G — already signed into the call and muted; and a plain telephone dial-in as the last resort, so the session can continue audio-only with slides driven from the room.

The important part is not the kit. It is that the backup was tested during rehearsal, and that everyone agreed in advance who decides to switch, and when.

## What the operator does during the session

A hybrid session needs a named operator whose only job is the remote link. During the session they:

- monitor the presenter's connection and switch to the backup at the first sign of sustained degradation, not after the freeze;
- ride the remote audio level so the presenter sits at the same loudness through the PA as an in-room voice;
- mute and unmute at the handovers, so coughs and keyboard noise never reach the room;
- manage the return feed and stay on talkback with whoever is calling the show;
- watch the platform chat, which is where a presenter quietly flags a problem.

"Set it and forget it" is how you get the frozen face. At EventSound we run hybrid links on Teams, Zoom, YouTube, Vimeo or a custom RTMP destination through broadcast-grade encoders, with redundant internet — a wired line backed by bonded cellular — so the remote link never hangs off venue wifi.

## What this means for your run of show

Remote segments need margin. Do not sandwich a remote keynote between two tight in-room items with zero slack. Practical adjustments cost nothing:

- allow a short buffer either side of each remote segment;
- group remote contributions where the format allows, so there are fewer handovers;
- brief the chair or MC with a holding script in case a reconnection takes a minute;
- decide in advance whether a dropped speaker rejoins immediately, at the next break, or not at all.

This is the same discipline that protects the rest of the day — see [the conference AV failure checklist](/blog/conference-av-failure-checklist/) for what a production team eliminates before doors open.

## A hybrid speaker checklist

- Platform confirmed and consistent with the production plan
- Connection test completed on that platform, headset in use
- Wired internet at the presenter's end where possible
- Two phone numbers collected and a dial-in fallback tested
- Rehearsal call covering handovers, slides, Q&A and the drop plan
- Return video agreed and tested
- Mix-minus confirmed with the AV supplier
- Named operator for the session
- Buffer time in the run of show around each remote segment
- Chair briefed with a holding script

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a remote speaker take live questions from the audience?**
Yes, provided every question reaches a microphone — a roving mic or a chair who repeats the question. The mix-minus feed carries the question to the presenter, and return video lets them see who is asking.

**What internet speed does the presenter need?**
Stability matters more than headline speed. A modest wired connection outperforms fast wifi that fluctuates. The venue side of the link is the bigger question — our guide to [live streaming an AGM or town hall](/blog/agm-town-hall-live-streaming/) covers venue connectivity in detail.

**Should the presenter share slides through the platform?**
Preferably not. Screen-sharing compresses the content and makes it hostage to their connection. Run the slides from playback in the room and have the presenter call the cues — cleaner picture, one less thing to fail.

**What happens if the speaker drops mid-sentence?**
With this workflow: the operator switches to the backup device or dial-in, the chair covers with the holding script, and the audience experiences a short pause. Without it: the long silence everyone remembers.
